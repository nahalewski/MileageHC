# TAKE CARE OF HER
## Chapter Four: The Morning After Snow

---

I woke at six to a room full of white light.

The fire was coals. The candles had burned to flat pools of wax on the table and on the mantel, each with its own small fossil of frozen flame. The library was cold in the particular way of rooms that had been warm and weren't any longer, the cold arriving from the floor up while the air held the last of the previous night's warmth above it. I lay still for a moment, orienting myself — the sofa, the cushion under my head, the blanket from God knows where draped over me, the white ceiling light which was not sunlight but the reflection of snow on the windows, the way it turns a room silver at dawn.

Julian was gone.

I had known he would be. He had a quality of discretion that was, I was understanding, even more thorough than I'd realized — the discretion of a man who had decided not to complicate things further by being present in the morning when they were already complicated enough at night. He had left the blanket. He had left the whisky glasses washed and turned on the draining board in the small bathroom adjoining the study, which I discovered later and which undid me briefly.

I lay for a while looking at the white ceiling.

The feeling in my body was strange and new and I examined it carefully, the way you examine an injury in the first minutes before it's decided what it wants to be. I was waiting for shame. I was waiting for the specific hot misery of having done something I was going to have to repent of, the sour morning-after taste of a decision made badly. I waited for some time. What arrived instead was quieter and harder to name — a rawness, like the feeling after crying, except I still had not cried. A kind of exposure. The specific vulnerability of having allowed something through that you have been keeping out for a very long time.

And underneath all of it, persistent and unreasonable: a warmth I didn't have words for yet.

I sat up slowly. The sofa cushions held the impression of both of us, which I noticed and looked at for a moment and then looked away from. The snow outside the east window was four inches at least on the stone sill, still falling lightly, the garden below it smooth and white and entirely transformed. I found my shoes. I folded the blanket. I did these things carefully and in order, which was my method for keeping myself functional when I was not certain of my own stability.

I went downstairs.

---

Cecile was in the kitchen, as she always was. It was six fifteen and she had been up since five — this was her constitution, nothing in the world was going to change it — and she was standing at the counter with a coffee she was actually drinking, which meant she hadn't yet begun the work of the morning proper. She turned when she heard me. Her face did the thing it sometimes did, the brief, comprehensive read of a person she knew very well, and then it settled into neutral.

"Good morning," she said.

"Good morning," I said.

She poured a second coffee and set it on the counter in front of the stool I always sat on. I sat on it. She went back to her own, looking out the window at the snow-covered kitchen garden.

We were quiet for a while. This was one of the things I loved about Cecile — she had an extraordinary tolerance for silence. She did not require the performance of conversation. She could sit in a room with a person and simply be in the room with a person, and the room was none the worse for it.

I wrapped my hands around the mug.

"He left early," I said.

"I heard the door at four-thirty," she said. Not accusatory. Not arch. Just: reporting.

"Cecile—"

"Eleanor." She turned to look at me. Her expression was exactly what I needed it to be, which was warm and direct and entirely without judgment, and I felt something loosen in my chest that I hadn't known was clenched. "You don't have to explain yourself to me."

"I know."

"Then don't."

I looked at the snow garden. A bird landed on the stone wall and looked around with the specific bewilderment of a creature that has found the world altered overnight.

"I stopped," I said. "In the middle. I saw the chair — Marcus's chair — and I just — stopped." I paused. "And then I started again. By choice. Very much by choice."

"I know," Cecile said.

"How do you know?"

"Because you are the kind of woman who makes choices clearly. Whatever you did last night, you did with your eyes open."

I considered this. "That's a generous framing."

"It's an accurate one." She refilled her coffee, though it didn't need refilling, which was her version of a fidget. "Are you all right?"

I thought about the question seriously, because Cecile asked serious questions and deserved serious answers. Was I all right? I was — raw. Exposed. Standing at the rim of something I had stepped into without being entirely prepared for the depth of it. I was also, beneath those things: yes. In a way I had not been in seventeen days.

"I think so," I said. "Ask me again in a week."

"I will," she said, and I knew she meant it.

The bird on the stone wall shook the snow off its wings and flew away.

"He loves you," Cecile said. Not tentatively — stating a fact she had known for a while and was now, for the first time, saying out loud.

"I know," I said.

"Have you always known?"

I thought about the drawer. Off its hinges now, the contents on the floor. "Mostly," I said. "I didn't examine it directly."

"Why not?"

"Because examining it directly would have required doing something about it, or deciding not to, and both of those were too complicated while Marcus was alive." I turned my mug in my hands. "And now Marcus isn't alive and somehow it's still complicated, just differently."

Cecile nodded slowly. "The different kind of complicated is better than the previous kind," she said. "In my opinion."

"Your opinion based on what?"

"Based on watching you for five years," she said, with the mild confidence of someone who has been paying attention while you were not paying attention to yourself. "You are more alive this morning than you were yesterday. You don't have to like that that's true. But it is."

I looked at her. She looked back, steady and warm, and I felt — for a brief and startling moment — the particular quality of Cecile's attention, which was different from Julian's and different from anyone else's: not desire exactly, not yet, not something I could name yet, but a tenderness so specific and so genuine that it sat in the room like a physical thing, like warmth.

I looked away.

"I'm going to go outside," I said.

---

I had not been properly outside since the morning in the garden with Thomas, a week ago. I had stood at windows. I had taken the air briefly from the back step. But I had not put on boots and gone out into the grounds — had not wanted to, had not had the interior resources for the kind of exposure that the outdoors requires. Today I wanted it. I needed the cold and the white and the particular silence that snow makes, the way it presses the world down to its essential shapes and strips out all the noise.

I found my coat in the entry hall. I found my boots. I wrapped a scarf around my neck twice and went out through the side door into the white.

The garden was extraordinary.

Marcus's roses — the canes, the careful winter architecture of them, the strict structure Thomas had cut them to in October — were all wearing snow, each one held in its white sleeve, the geometry of them preserved and emphasized by the covering. The paths between the beds were smooth and unbroken. The stone wall at the far boundary was a long white line against the pale sky. The oak tree in the north corner had become something else entirely, its enormous spread now a cathedral of white branches against gray.

I stood at the edge of the flagstone terrace and breathed in the cold air and felt it in my lungs with a clarity that was almost painful, the way returning to something sharp feels after time in the muffled and the numbed.

Thomas was already there.

Of course he was — it was barely seven and he was already in the garden, at the far end of the rose beds, doing something methodical with a length of burlap around one of the larger established plants. He hadn't seen me yet. I watched him work for a moment: the economy of his movements, nothing wasted, hands that knew exactly what they were doing in the way that hands do when they have performed the same work hundreds of times. His breath made slow white clouds in the air. He was wearing the green wax jacket and a dark scarf and the flat-brimmed hat he wore for cold weather, which gave him a quality of being exactly right for the landscape, like something that belonged in the composition of it.

He looked up.

He looked at me for a moment across the white garden with the gray sky behind him and didn't say anything. He raised one hand. I raised mine. Then he went back to the rose.

I walked out.

The snow was deep enough to come over the top of my boots in the beds if I stepped off the path, so I stayed on the flagstone paths, which Thomas had swept clear at some point this morning without being asked. I walked the south path and then the east path and came around to where he was working and stood near him without speaking because I had learned, in the weeks since Marcus died, that Thomas did not require speech to make a presence meaningful.

He finished wrapping the burlap and tied it off and stood up and looked at the work and then looked at me with an expression of — assessment. Not personal assessment. The look he gave things in the garden when he was determining their state. A looking at.

"You slept in the library," he said.

"Yes."

"Cold this morning."

"Very." I paused. "How long have you been out here?"

"Since five-thirty." He said it without apology or self-congratulation, the way you'd report the weather. "Wanted to see what the snow did to the beds before anyone walked through them."

"And?"

He considered. "It's a good snow. Heavy enough to protect but not enough to damage the frames." He nodded toward the greenhouse at the far end, which was steam-misted from within, its amber warmth visible through the glass. "The heat lamps stayed on."

"Good," I said.

We stood in the snow for a moment. A small wind came through the garden, picking up a drift from the stone wall and sending it in a slow, horizontal scatter across the white surface. I watched it and Thomas watched it and then it was done.

"I'll help," I said. "If there's anything I can do."

He looked at me. "In that coat?"

"I won't melt."

Something moved in his face that was not quite a smile but was in the family of smiles — the particular restrained expression of someone who finds something welcome but isn't going to perform their welcome. "There's brush to clear from the south terrace. If you want to start there."

He passed me a small hand broom from the basket near the path without ceremony. I took it. He went back to the burlap.

---

We worked in silence for the better part of an hour.

I cleared snow and brush from the south terrace's stone urns while Thomas moved methodically through the rose beds, checking each wrapped plant, pulling back the burlap where it had shifted in the night and resecuring it. Every now and then I was aware of him in my peripheral vision — the dark shape of him against the white, the unhurried deliberateness. He did not check on me or comment on my work. He trusted that if I was doing something wrong I would figure it out or ask.

This was, I realized, a quality I had been missing without naming it: being trusted to occupy space without supervision.

Marcus had loved me with the kind of attention that was also a form of monitoring. Not malicious — he had never been malicious — but thorough, the way he was thorough about everything. He noticed everything I did and most of what I felt, and his noticing had a weight to it, a quality of assessment, and I had spent five years understanding that I was always being seen and had developed around that seeing a particular posture, a particular management of myself that I had not known was there until this morning in the garden, using a hand broom in someone else's space with no one watching how I did it.

Thomas was not watching how I did it.

He was aware of me — I was certain of that. He had a quality of environmental awareness that meant he always knew what was happening in his periphery, which was a gardener's instinct and also something more personal. But he was not watching me the way someone watches something they're managing. He was watching me the way you watch weather — present to it, registering it, letting it be what it was.

I swept snow off the last of the urns and stood with the broom and looked at the garden.

The snow had stopped. The sky was lightening now, pulling from gray toward the thin white of a winter morning, and the garden was everything at once — the white, the dormant shapes of things, the skeleton architecture of the beds without their summer growth. Beautiful in a way that required the stripping. The garden was most itself like this, I thought — reduced to structure, to what was permanent.

"Can I ask you something?" I said.

Thomas straightened from the far end of the bed and looked at me. "Yes."

"What did Marcus's letter say? Not the specifics. Just — the shape of it."

He was quiet for a moment. He came around the end of the bed and stood near me, looking at the same stretch of garden I was looking at. Not at me. Which was how Thomas answered hard questions — shoulder to shoulder with the thing rather than face to face with it.

"It said to be here," he said. "To pay attention. To let you lead."

"That's all?"

He was quiet again, longer this time. "He said you needed someone who could be present without making it into something you owed them."

I felt the truth of this like a physical sensation, something pressing against my sternum from the inside. "He knew that about me."

"He knew a lot about you."

"He did," I said. "He knew a lot about me and he used most of it to love me and some of it to hold me in place." I said this with the flat precision of someone who has been working their way toward a sentence for a while and has finally found it. "Both of those things were true at the same time. I'm still working out what to do with that."

Thomas nodded slowly. He was not going to comment on it, I knew. He would receive it and hold it and that would be sufficient.

"He trusted you," I said. "All three of you. Cecile and Julian and you. He trusted you with — with me. Which is either the most loving thing or the most—" I stopped. "I don't have the right word."

"Controlling," Thomas said.

I looked at him. He met my eyes with the steady directness that was his resting state, no apology in it, no softening.

"Yes," I said. "Controlling."

"Those aren't mutually exclusive," he said. "In my experience."

"No," I said. "They're not."

We stood in the white garden for a moment. The light was coming up properly now, the low winter sun finding the top of the stone wall and gilding it. The greenhouse at the far end glowed with its own internal warmth.

"Thank you," I said. "For sweeping the paths this morning."

He shrugged — not dismissively, but with the specific body language of someone for whom acknowledgment is fine but not required. "They needed doing."

"You didn't have to."

"I know."

That was it. He picked up the basket and moved to the next section of beds and I stood for a moment watching him go, the dark shape of him against all that white, and I felt something that was not desire and was not grief and was not gratitude exactly, though it was related to all three. Something quieter than any of those words. A recognition, maybe. The specific feeling of being in the presence of someone who sees you accurately and does not require you to be otherwise.

I had been married to a man who saw me accurately and required something different anyway.

I turned that over, standing in the snow, watching Thomas work. He moved the way he always moved — without hurry, without performance, entirely native to the task and the environment, as though the garden were an extension of his body and he was simply doing what his body did. He glanced back once and found me still standing there and said nothing, just nodded slightly, and went back to work.

I went back to the terrace.

---

We worked until nine, when Cecile appeared at the side door and called out that breakfast was ready and would be cold in twenty minutes. Thomas stacked his tools. I returned the hand broom to the basket without being asked and he noticed that I'd done it without comment, which was the Thomas version of approval.

We walked back to the house through the snow. The paths he'd cleared at five-thirty were filling again slightly, a new thin layer of white settling over the swept stone, and Thomas walked beside me with the unhurried quality of someone with no intention of being somewhere other than exactly where he was.

At the side door he stopped. He wasn't coming in — he rarely came in for meals, had a habit of eating in the greenhouse or the potting shed, which was apparently how he liked it and which Marcus had respected without fully understanding.

"Same time tomorrow," he said. "If you want."

"What time is same time?"

"Seven. Earlier if you want to see the garden before I've touched it."

I looked at him. He was looking at the garden rather than at me, already calculating the next day's work.

"Six-thirty," I said.

Something shifted in his face. The not-quite-smile. "I'll be in the greenhouse if it's cold."

"I'll find you," I said.

I went inside.

---

Cecile had made eggs and toast and had set the table in the kitchen rather than the dining room, which was the correct instinct. The dining room required a mood I didn't have. The kitchen required only sitting down.

I ate and she sat across from me with her coffee and we were quiet in the good way.

"Thomas said Marcus told him to be here," I said eventually. "Present without making it something I owed."

Cecile looked at her coffee. "That sounds like Marcus's letter."

"What does yours say?"

She looked at me for a moment. "That you need tenderness without a claim attached to it," she said carefully. "And that I've always had that available for you and you haven't been free to fully receive it."

I was quiet.

"He was right," I said.

"Yes," she said.

"That's—" I stopped. "I don't know what to do with the fact that he saw all of it, clearly, and arranged it from the outside. It feels like—" I pressed my fingers to the table. "It feels like being understood and being managed at the same time."

"Because it is both of those things," Cecile said. "In equal measure. That was Marcus."

"Yes," I said. "That was Marcus."

I looked at the kitchen garden through the window, still white, still quiet. The bird from this morning was back on the stone wall, more confident now that the snow had settled and the world had decided on its shape for the day.

I thought about Julian's hand warm against my back. I thought about Thomas in the garden, his breath making slow white clouds, his hands sure on the burlap. I thought about Cecile's coffee and the specific quality of her eyes when she looked at me — warm and direct and without agenda.

I thought about Marcus, writing three letters at his desk.

I thought: *You arranged the room. Again. You always arranged the room.*

And then, quieter, the thought that surprised me: *And this time, for the first time, you arranged it around what I needed rather than what you needed to give.*

That was not the same thing. It had not, in our marriage, always been the same thing.

I picked up my fork and finished my eggs.

Outside, the snow was perfect and white and still, and the garden waited under it with all its structures intact, dormant and patient, knowing that whatever was covered would eventually grow back in its own time, in its own form, toward whatever light found it.

I was starting to believe that was true of more than gardens.

---

At eleven I went back to the library and sat in my usual place on the sofa and looked at Marcus's chair across from me.

Julian had sat in it yesterday. He had sat in it with his feet flat on the floor and his hands on the arms and the specific deliberateness of a man who had decided to stop treating that chair as off-limits, and I had watched him do it and felt the room rearrange itself around the choice.

The chair was just a chair. It held whoever sat in it. It had no loyalty.

I was working, slowly and without a fixed destination, toward a version of grief that could hold contradictions without needing to resolve them. Marcus had loved me. He had also controlled me, quietly and thoroughly, for five years. Both of those things were true and neither of them was the complete story and he was gone now and had left behind three people who were navigating his instructions with varying degrees of conflict, and I was sitting in the middle of all of it with six unopened envelopes in the cabinet by the window.

I got up and went to the cabinet.

I took out the second envelope. I sat back down.

The fire needed stoking. I left it for the moment.

I held the envelope in both hands the way Cecile had held hers in the doorway — the weight of it, the specific gravity of Marcus's handwriting on the front, the dark wax seal with his initials pressed into it.

I broke it.

He began with love. He always began with love. *Ellie.* Just that, at the top of the page, my name in his handwriting, and already something in my chest was doing its complicated thing.

I read slowly, the way you read things that require all of you.

Outside the window the snow had stopped and the garden was still and Thomas was gone home and somewhere in the city Julian was in his flat with the letter still in his jacket pocket, I suspected, warm against his chest.

The second letter was longer than I expected. It went on for three pages and it told me things Marcus had never said out loud and some things I had already understood and some things I had not known were true.

I read it twice.

Then I sat for a long time in the library with the letter in my lap and looked at the empty chair and thought about what it means to be loved by someone who is no longer there to be angry at, and whether anger and love can live in the same space without one of them winning, and whether the answer to that question was yes or only *eventually.*

Eventually, I thought, was a place I was beginning to move toward.

That was something. It was, I thought, enough for today.

I folded the second letter and put it in the pocket of my cardigan, against my chest, and picked up my book, which I still wasn't reading, and sat in the white winter light while the house breathed quietly around me and the garden waited outside and everything was, for the first time in a very long while, entirely still.

---

*End of Chapter Four*
