# TAKE CARE OF HER
### A Novel Concept Package
**Genre:** Dark Psychological Romance / Gothic Estate Erotic Drama
**Target Audience:** Adult readers of dark romance, BookTok literary fiction, gothic erotic drama

---

## PART ONE: ONE-PAGE BOOK SYNOPSIS

Eleanor Voss is thirty-one years old when her husband dies in a hotel room two thousand miles away. She doesn't cry at the funeral. She stands in black silk on a gray October morning and accepts condolences from people who knew her as *Marcus's wife* and wonders, somewhere beneath the numbness, who she is now.

Three days later, the letters arrive.

Not to her. To them — Cecile, the maid who has managed the Voss estate for seven years. Thomas, the gardener who knows every hour of the grounds by its light. And Julian, Marcus's closest friend, the man who gave a toast at their wedding and could not quite meet Eleanor's eyes while he gave it.

The envelopes are sealed in cream vellum, addressed in Marcus's handwriting. Each bears the same instruction on the front: *Take Care of Her.*

Eleanor assumes the letters are practical. Financial guidance. Emergency contacts. Instructions about the house. She is wrong.

As the weeks fold into winter, Eleanor begins to notice a change in the three people around her — a tenderness with intention behind it, a watchfulness that feels almost devotional. Cecile begins leaving wildflowers on her breakfast tray. Thomas works in the garden even in rain, and when Eleanor watches from the window he does not pretend not to see her. Julian calls every evening at eight o'clock, precisely, and asks her nothing about the estate.

When Eleanor finally demands to read the letters, she discovers that Marcus planned beyond grief. He had loved her with an obsessive, clear-eyed thoroughness, and he had known something about her that she hadn't admitted to herself: that she was lonely inside their marriage long before she was lonely outside it. That she had needs she had never named. That she might, if she allowed herself, choose to feel again.

The letters are an invitation. Not a command. Never a command — Marcus had been meticulous about that, too.

What follows is Eleanor's reckoning with a love that was both beautiful and controlling, a desire she carries like a wound, and three people who are navigating their own impossible instructions. The gardener offers silence and presence. The maid offers truth and tenderness. The best friend offers everything he should have offered years ago — and knows exactly how wrong that is.

*Take Care of Her* is a slow-burn exploration of grief as a physical state, desire as a form of loyalty, and the question that haunts every page: when a dead man arranges your healing, is the choice still yours?

## PART TWO: CHARACTER PROFILES

---

### ELEANOR VOSS — *The Widow*
**Age:** 31
**Physical Description:** Dark hair worn long, pale olive skin, gray-green eyes with a quality of appraising stillness. She moves through rooms like she's testing their weight. She is beautiful in the way that serious women are beautiful — not immediately, but completely, once you've looked long enough.

**Background:** Eleanor came from modest circumstances and met Marcus Voss at twenty-four when she was completing a graduate degree in architectural history. She married him at twenty-six. She has always been intelligent, self-possessed, and privately uncertain whether love or security drove her to accept his proposal. She stopped asking that question when the asking became too uncomfortable.

**Emotional State at Story Open:** Dissociated grief. Guilt — not because she didn't love Marcus, but because she is aware of the complicated texture of her love. She did not know all of what he had arranged. She is unsure whether she would have wanted to know.

**Agency & Arc:** Eleanor's arc is the spine of the novel. She begins numb, moves through anger (at Marcus's control, at her own longing), and arrives — through her own deliberate choices — at something she can call her own. Every intimate moment in the story is one she chooses, steps toward, and can step away from. Her healing is not a transaction. It is a reclamation.

**Voice:** First-person, interior, lyrical. Precise about objects, evasive about feelings until she isn't.

---

### MARCUS VOSS — *The Husband*
**Age:** Would have been 38
**Physical Description:** Described through Eleanor's memory: tall, silver-templed early, a voice that filled rooms without effort. He dressed with care. He remembered everything.

**Background:** Self-made, the first of his family to become genuinely wealthy, Marcus channeled that drive into his marriage with the same focused intensity. He loved Eleanor absolutely. He also loved her the way some people love rare, irreplaceable things — with a possessiveness that isn't quite peace.

**Presence in the Narrative:** Marcus never appears in real time. He lives in letters, in a series of video recordings he left behind, in the habits of everyone who orbited him. His voice in the letters is warm, precise, and occasionally unsettling. He is at once the most loving and the most controlling character in the book.

**Moral Ambiguity:** The central question about Marcus is whether the letters represent profound love or profound arrogance — whether planning your wife's emotional recovery is a gift or a violation. The novel does not resolve this cleanly. Eleanor does.

---

### JULIAN ASHWORTH — *The Best Friend*
**Age:** 37
**Physical Description:** Lean, dark-eyed, slightly rumpled in the way of men who went to expensive schools and stopped caring. A jaw that looks like it was carved under pressure. Hands that are always doing something — turning a glass, testing a doorframe.

**Background:** Julian met Marcus at university. He has been in the periphery of Eleanor and Marcus's marriage for its entirety. He gave the toast at their wedding. He was the one who called Eleanor from the hospital. He is the only person in the world who loved Marcus and is also in love with Marcus's wife, and he has carried that in silence for five years with the specific self-discipline of a man who knows exactly what he is and refuses to become worse.

**Internal Conflict:** Julian's chapter voice is close-third, controlled, full of things he does not say. He read the letter. He told himself he wouldn't follow its instructions. He has called Eleanor every evening at eight o'clock for six weeks.

**Arc:** From controlled denial to honest reckoning with his own heart, his guilt, and what it means to honor a dead man by living.

---

### THOMAS VANE — *The Gardener*
**Age:** 34
**Physical Description:** Built from outdoor work — broad through the shoulders, hands like geography, a face that has been weathered into a kind of handsomeness that photographs would miss. He speaks slowly and means every word.

**Background:** Thomas has managed the Voss estate grounds for four years. He is a man of few social complications — he knows what he feels, doesn't see the need to say it, and lives close to the ground in the literal sense. He is the first person to notice when Eleanor is about to break and the last person to say so.

**Role in Eleanor's Arc:** Thomas offers physical safety and wordless companionship. Their intimacy, when it develops, is grounded, present-tense, and deeply consensual — built from shared mornings and honest silences.

**Voice:** Limited third-person. Short declarative sentences. Noticing things.

---

### CECILE MARTIN — *The Maid*
**Age:** 42
**Physical Description:** French-Haitian, elegant in a practical way, a woman who has mastered the art of being entirely herself inside someone else's house. She wears her silver-streaked hair in a low twist. She has a laugh she uses rarely and a directness she uses often.

**Background:** Cecile has worked in the Voss household for seven years and has watched the marriage from the inside. She knows which drawers Marcus kept locked. She knows which mornings Eleanor came downstairs with red-rimmed eyes. She loved Marcus for his generosity and was sometimes angry at him on Eleanor's behalf.

**Role in Eleanor's Arc:** Cecile is the truth-teller. She is the first person Eleanor really talks to, the first person who says plainly that grief doesn't follow rules and desire isn't a betrayal. Their intimacy is tender, emotionally charged, and genuine. It cracks Eleanor open more than anything else.

**Voice:** First-person sections (journal entries), close-third in her POV chapters. Warm, direct, wry.

## PART THREE: 24-CHAPTER OUTLINE

---

### ACT ONE: THE INSTRUCTIONS (Chapters 1–7)
*The funeral. The letters. The household holding its breath.*

---

**CHAPTER 1: "Three Days After"**
*POV: Eleanor (first person)*

The funeral is over. Eleanor moves through the marble-floored house like a woman learning to walk in a different gravity. She doesn't sleep in their bedroom. She has migrated to the library — she tells herself it's because the bed is too big, but the real reason is that she can smell him there and it is both unbearable and necessary. Cecile brings her tea she doesn't drink. Thomas has been working the south garden despite the rain. Julian called at eight, and she didn't answer, and he called again at eight the next night, and the night after.

On the third day, she watches from the window as Cecile receives an envelope. Then sees Thomas accept one from the estate lawyer's courier. Then Julian's name in Marcus's handwriting, sent to his flat in the city. She notes this with the distant precision of someone cataloguing evidence she doesn't yet understand.

*Emotional purpose: Establish Eleanor's dissociated grief, the atmosphere of the estate, and the mystery of the letters without revealing their content.*
*Tension level: 1/5*
*Ending hook: "I told myself the letters were about the house. I was almost right about that."*

---

**CHAPTER 2: "What the Letters Say"**
*POV: Julian (close third)*

Julian reads his letter alone in his flat. Marcus's handwriting fills the page — three pages, actually. Julian reads it twice, folds it carefully, and puts it in the inside pocket of his jacket. Then he sits for a long time looking at nothing. The letter is specific. It is also, Julian thinks, absolutely insane. And also — he cannot stop thinking about it — the most loving thing Marcus ever did and the most presumptuous. He calls Eleanor at eight o'clock. She doesn't answer. He will call tomorrow.

Meanwhile, we see Cecile reading her letter in her room above the kitchen, and Thomas reading his at the workbench in the greenhouse. Their reactions are different in texture but identical in conclusion: *Marcus knew things about Eleanor that she didn't know about herself. And he trusted us with those things. And now we have to decide what to do.*

*Emotional purpose: Reveal the existence of the letters' deeper purpose without fully disclosing their content to the reader yet. Establish each character's moral conflict.*
*Tension level: 1/5*
*Ending hook: Julian locks the letter in his glove compartment and drives to the Voss estate. He has no idea what he's going to say.*

---

**CHAPTER 3: "The Inventory"**
*POV: Eleanor (first person)*

Eleanor decides to take an inventory of her life. It is a coping mechanism — she needs tasks with edges. She catalogs the house, room by room. This chapter moves slowly through the estate: the way morning light comes through the conservatory glass, the smell of Marcus's study (sandalwood, paper, old ambition), the locked cabinet in the bedroom that she has never needed to open because Marcus always handled whatever was in it. She finds things she forgot she owned. Photographs she didn't know Marcus kept. A bottle of wine from their first year, set aside for an anniversary that will not come.

At the end of the day, she sits in the kitchen with Cecile and asks, for the first time, directly: *What was in your letter?* Cecile's answer is careful and partial. She says Marcus asked her to stay close to Eleanor. That the estate matters, yes, but that Eleanor herself matters more. It sounds like a reasonable answer. It does not feel complete.

*Emotional purpose: Introduce the estate as a character. Deepen Eleanor and Cecile's relationship. Plant the seed of Eleanor's suspicion that the letters contain more than she's been told.*
*Tension level: 1/5*
*Ending hook: The locked cabinet. She finds the key on his keychain. She doesn't open it yet.*

---

**CHAPTER 4: "The Gardener in the Rain"**
*POV: Thomas (close third)*

Thomas has a problem. He is not good at ignoring things, and Eleanor has started standing at the library window in the mornings, and Thomas notices her every time, and he is not sure whether the right thing to do is acknowledge this or give her the privacy of pretending he doesn't see. He decides to see her. He raises his hand once, briefly, like a fact. She raises hers back.

By the end of the chapter, Eleanor comes outside for the first time since the funeral. Thomas is replanting the winter beds. She asks if she can help. He says yes and hands her a trowel without performance. They work in silence for an hour. When she goes back inside, Thomas leans on the fence and watches the rain come in and thinks about Marcus's letter and what it means that he is already doing the thing he was asked to do without having decided to.

*Emotional purpose: Introduce physical grounding as a form of healing. Establish the Eleanor-Thomas dynamic as present-tense, sensory, non-verbal.*
*Tension level: 2/5*
*Ending hook: Thomas reads the letter again that night. The word Marcus used was *tenderness.* That's the word that's the problem.*

---

**CHAPTER 5: "Eight O'Clock"**
*POV: Eleanor (first person)*

Eleanor answers Julian's call. She isn't sure why — maybe because it's the sixth night in a row and the precision of it has started to feel like something she can rely on, which is a thing she is short on. Julian talks to her for forty-seven minutes about nothing important: a documentary he watched, a restaurant that closed, an anecdote about Marcus from university that she hasn't heard. He doesn't mention the letter. She doesn't ask. Before he hangs up he says *I'll call tomorrow,* and she says *I know.*

She sleeps in the library again, but this time she doesn't smell Marcus in the air. She smells rain from the garden, where Thomas left a window open, and Cecile's coffee from the kitchen below. She wonders when she started cataloguing the house by its people rather than its corners.

*Emotional purpose: Establish Julian as a consistent, warm presence. Begin Eleanor's unconscious turn toward the living.*
*Tension level: 2/5*
*Ending hook: She finds a handwritten note tucked into the book she was reading. Marcus's handwriting. *You will be alright, Ellie. I made sure of it.* She doesn't know when he put it there.*

---

**CHAPTER 6: "What the House Remembers"**
*POV: Cecile (journal entry, first person)*

Cecile writes in her journal about the night Eleanor found the note, because Cecile saw her face when she found it and couldn't sleep after. The chapter is Cecile's private meditation on seven years of watching this marriage — the beauty of it, the weight of it, the things she knew Marcus did without telling Eleanor. He wasn't cruel. He was thorough. There is a difference, but sometimes the difference is hard to describe from the outside.

Cecile writes about what her letter asked of her: to be present, to be honest, to watch for the moment Eleanor is ready to name what she needs — and then to name it with her. Not for her. *With her.* Cecile underlines this. Marcus underlined it too.

*Emotional purpose: Provide interiority and moral clarity about the letter's intent. Introduce Cecile as the novel's ethical compass.*
*Tension level: 1/5*
*Ending hook: "She found the note. She won't sleep tonight. I'll leave the kitchen light on."*

---

**CHAPTER 7: "The Locked Cabinet"**
*POV: Eleanor (first person)*

Eleanor opens the cabinet. Inside: financial documents (as expected), insurance information (as expected), a USB drive (unexpected), and a series of numbered envelopes addressed to Eleanor in Marcus's handwriting. Not like the letters sent to the others. These are for her. There is a note paper-clipped to the front of the stack: *In order. Only when you're ready. Don't rush, Ellie. I'll wait.*

She counts seven envelopes. She takes the first one and locks the rest back inside. She sits at the library desk and opens it with a letter opener, in lamplight, while rain hits the windows. The first letter is about love — how he loved her, when he knew it, why he never quite knew how to say it at the volume she deserved. It ends with: *I know I held things tight. Some things I held too tight. The next letters will explain. But start here: you were the best thing in my life, and I was imperfect with you, and I'm sorry.*

Eleanor closes the letter. She does not cry. But she finally goes back to sleep in their bedroom.

*Emotional purpose: End Act One with Eleanor taking her first active step. Marcus's complexity begins to surface. The mystery deepens.*
*Tension level: 2/5*
*Ending hook: Seven envelopes. One opened. Six waiting. The USB drive, untouched.*

---

### ACT TWO: WHAT THE INSTRUCTIONS MEAN (Chapters 8–18)
*The widow asks questions. The others struggle. Intimacy builds, slowly and honestly.*

---

**CHAPTER 8: "The Second Letter"**
*POV: Eleanor (first person)*

Three weeks pass. Eleanor has developed a rhythm: mornings in the garden with Thomas, evenings on the phone with Julian, and the quiet hours between that belong to Cecile and the house. She opens the second letter on a Tuesday. Marcus, in this letter, is more direct. He writes about their marriage with an honesty she never heard from him in life — the ways he knew he was controlling, the ways he justified it, the fact that he watched her grow smaller in certain rooms and didn't know how to stop demanding the space she was giving up.

He also writes, for the first time, that the people around her have instructions. He doesn't say what the instructions are. He says: *They know what you need better than you'll admit to yourself right now. Let them show you. You don't have to do anything you don't want to do. That's the whole point, Ellie. You never have to do anything you don't want to do.*

Eleanor reads the letter four times. Then she goes to find Cecile.

*Emotional purpose: Eleanor begins to understand the scope of what Marcus planned. First confrontation between Eleanor and Cecile about the letters.*
*Tension level: 2/5*
*Ending hook: Cecile tells her the truth — partially. Eleanor says: "Tell me all of it." Cecile says: "Are you sure?" Eleanor says: "I've been sure my whole life. That hasn't always served me. Ask me again tomorrow."*

---

**CHAPTER 9: "Telling Her"**
*POV: Cecile (close third)*

The next morning, Eleanor comes down early. She sits at the kitchen table with her hands around a mug and says: *All of it, Cecile.* So Cecile tells her. Not everything in the letter — some of it is hers — but the shape of what Marcus intended. That he wanted the three of them to be more than caretakers. That he wanted Eleanor to have people around her who were *paying attention.* That he wanted her to be seen. And if Eleanor ever wanted more than being seen — more than company, more than presence — he wanted her to have the freedom to reach for it.

Cecile watches Eleanor's face during this. She is trained at reading that face. The expressions that pass across it, in order: disbelief, anger, grief, something startled and almost amused, and finally a stillness that is not the same as the numbness she arrived with.

*Eleanor says: "He arranged for me to be — what, comforted? Seduced? After his death?" Cecile says: "He arranged for you to have permission. The rest is yours."*

*Emotional purpose: The central premise is fully disclosed. Eleanor's reaction sets the moral tone — she is not devastated or resigned; she is engaged, curious, and angry in the productive way.*
*Tension level: 2/5*
*Ending hook: "And what about you?" Eleanor asks. "Are you following his instructions right now?" Cecile meets her eyes. "I was following them before I got the letter."*

---

**CHAPTER 10: "Distance"**
*POV: Julian (close third)*

Julian drives to the estate. He tells himself it's because Eleanor asked him to come after speaking with Cecile. But he is also aware, with the precision he applies to all things that threaten him, that he has been in love with Eleanor Voss for five years and the only thing that has ever been successfully between that feeling and his actions is Marcus.

The visit is careful. Eleanor is different — not in a way anyone else would see, but Julian sees. She's less careful with herself. She sits in the window seat with her knees folded up, which she would never have done before. She asks him directly: *Did Marcus ask you to love me?* Julian takes a long moment. Then he says: *He asked me to take care of you. I think he knew the distinction was complicated for me.*

*Emotional purpose: First explicit acknowledgment of Julian's feelings. The tension between honor, grief, and desire is laid bare.*
*Tension level: 3/5*
*Ending hook: Eleanor says goodnight and goes upstairs. Julian drives home. He calls Cecile at midnight to ask if she said anything to Eleanor. Cecile says yes. Julian says: "How was she?" Cecile says: "Honest. For the first time in a long time. She was honest."*

---

**CHAPTER 11: "The Greenhouse"**
*POV: Thomas (close third)*

Thomas is not a man who lives in his head. He noticed, however, that Eleanor was different when she came to the garden this morning. Looser. Her posture carried something released. He didn't ask. She didn't explain. They worked the winter beds together and he showed her how to read the soil and she listened with her whole body, the way she listens, and Thomas thought about Marcus's letter and thought: *this is the part I can do. This part I already know.*

The chapter culminates at dusk, in the greenhouse. Eleanor is pressing seedlings into trays. The glass holds the last light. She says, without looking up: *Did he tell you I was lonely?* Thomas doesn't answer right away. Then he says: *He told me you needed someone to notice. I would have noticed anyway.* Eleanor laughs — the first real laugh since the funeral, surprised out of her like something she forgot she owned.

*Emotional purpose: Physical safety, sensory intimacy, and genuine laughter. The Eleanor-Thomas dynamic is grounded and healing without being rushed.*
*Tension level: 3/5*
*Ending hook: She stays in the greenhouse until it's dark. Thomas drives home. He does not tell himself there's nothing there.*

---

**CHAPTER 12: "Letter Three"**
*POV: Eleanor (first person)*

The third letter from the cabinet. Marcus writes about intimacy — what it is, how he often got it wrong, the specific ways he understands now (too late) that Eleanor needed to feel desired rather than possessed. He writes about loneliness inside a marriage as the particular kind that has no shape you can show anyone. He writes: *I know you felt it. I know I caused some of it. I know also that you are the kind of woman who will feel guilty for wanting comfort after I am gone. I am writing to tell you: please don't. Whatever you feel, whatever you want — those feelings do not make you unfaithful to me. They make you alive. That's all I ever wanted for you.*

Eleanor sets the letter down. She pours a glass of wine. She takes it to the window and watches the dark garden for a long time, where the path lights outline the beds Thomas built and the greenhouse holds the last warmth.

*Emotional purpose: Marcus's voice at its most loving and its most psychologically sophisticated. Eleanor is moved and unsettled in equal measure — this is too much knowledge, too late, from a man who can't answer for it.*
*Tension level: 2/5*
*Ending hook: She calls Julian at 11:43 PM. He answers on the first ring.*

---

**CHAPTER 13: "What We Don't Say"**
*POV: Julian (close third)*

Julian and Eleanor talk until 2 AM. Not about the letters. Not directly. They talk about Marcus, and about the years before, and at some point they talk about the night of Eleanor's thirtieth birthday when Marcus was away and Julian brought her dinner because she didn't want to celebrate alone. They've never talked about that night. There's nothing to tell — they ate dinner and he went home at a reasonable hour. But there is something about the memory that they both approach carefully.

Julian says: *I should tell you something.* Eleanor says: *I already know.* Julian says: *You can't know.* Eleanor says: *You've been in love with me. Marcus knew. He put it in the letter.* A long silence. Then Julian says: *I would never have—* Eleanor says: *I know. That's why he trusted you with this.*

*Emotional purpose: The secret is out. Relief and new tension. The ethics of the situation are now fully visible to both of them.*
*Tension level: 4/5*
*Ending hook: "Then what happens now?" Julian asks. Eleanor says: "I don't know yet. But I'm the one who decides. Marcus made sure of that." She can hear Julian breathe. "Yes," he says. "He did."*

---

**CHAPTER 14: "Cecile's Truth"**
*POV: Cecile (journal entry, first person)*

Cecile writes about her own feelings for Eleanor with unusual directness. She has never been a woman who hid from herself, and she is not hiding now: she loves Eleanor. She loved her before the letter and she'll love her after, and the letter didn't create anything that wasn't already true. What the letter did was give her permission she hadn't known she needed.

She writes about the specific tenderness of living in someone's house — the intimacy of small details, the knowing of a person through their habits and moods and the objects they carry. She writes about what it means to be trusted with someone's grief and whether it is ethical to want more than to be trusted.

Then she writes: *She told me she read Marcus's third letter. She said 'He knew he failed me.' I said, 'He loved you.' She said, 'Those aren't mutually exclusive.' I said nothing, because she was right, and sometimes the right answer is just to let someone be right.*

*Emotional purpose: Deepen Cecile's interiority. Introduce the Eleanor-Cecile romantic tension as something Eleanor is beginning to perceive.*
*Tension level: 3/5*
*Ending hook: "She put her hand on mine at dinner. She didn't move it for a long time. I didn't either."*

---

**CHAPTER 15: "The Storm"**
*POV: Eleanor (first person)*

A winter storm knocks the power out at the estate. The chapter is intimate and sensory: candlelight, the sound of the storm on the old house, Cecile and Eleanor wrapped in blankets on the library sofa reading by candlelight, Thomas banging something back into place on the roof while the wind howls. Julian drives out — Eleanor texted him without overthinking it — and arrives at eleven o'clock damp and windswept and slightly absurd.

The four of them end up in the kitchen, which is the warmest room. Wine, candles, a meal improvised from what Cecile finds. The storm chapters are the first time the full ensemble is together in intimate space, and the result is tension wrapped inside warmth — laughter, accidental touches, the electricity of proximity. Eleanor is aware of every point where her body is near someone else's.

At the end, the power comes back. The lights are garish after the candlelight. Eleanor blinks in the sudden brightness and something she was just beginning to name slips away. *It will come back,* she thinks. *Whatever that was, it will come back.*

*Emotional purpose: Gothic atmosphere, ensemble warmth, and the first sustained multi-character tension scene. Eleanor becomes aware of her own desire as a physical fact.*
*Tension level: 4/5*
*Ending hook: Julian is the last one to leave. He stands in the doorway for a moment. Eleanor says his name and he turns. She says: "Nothing. Drive safe." But it wasn't nothing.*

---

**CHAPTER 16: "Letter Four"**
*POV: Eleanor (first person)*

The fourth letter is harder to read. Marcus writes about jealousy — his, specifically, and how aware he was that it colored the way he loved her. He writes about Julian. Not with anger, but with the careful honesty of a man writing his own accounting. He writes: *I knew Julian was in love with you. I kept you both close and I kept you apart, and I called it friendship when part of it was control. That was wrong. I'm sorry for it.* He writes about Thomas and Cecile with a specificity that stuns Eleanor — he knew everything, saw everything, understood more about the people in his household than he ever admitted.

He writes: *You deserve to be loved by people who can show you. I couldn't always. Others can. I'm asking you to let them.*

Eleanor finishes the letter and sits still for a very long time. Then she opens the locked cabinet and looks at the remaining envelopes. Three left. And the USB drive.

*Emotional purpose: Marcus's culpability becomes clearer. The permission he's giving feels earned and complicated. Eleanor's anger surfaces.*
*Tension level: 2/5*
*Ending hook: She picks up the USB drive. Not yet. She puts it back. But she runs her thumb across the edge of it twice before she closes the cabinet.*

---

**CHAPTER 17: "What Grief Leaves Room For"**
*POV: Thomas (close third)*

Thomas has started leaving things for Eleanor to find — not obviously, not intrusively. A tea rose from the south garden on the library windowsill. A fire already built in the library grate when she comes down early. He is not good with words and he does not try to be something he isn't. He knows what attention looks like when it's quiet.

This chapter: Eleanor comes to the greenhouse early and finds Thomas doing the slow work of the winter month — cataloguing seeds, mending trays. She asks if she can sit. He says yes. She sits on the old bench along the far wall and watches him work, and something in the particular quality of that morning — the low light, the smell of earth and glass, his steadiness — breaks something loose in her.

She tells him she's been reading Marcus's letters. He listens. She says: *Did he tell you about the loneliness?* Thomas says: *He told me you needed someone to be here.* She says: *That's not the same thing.* He meets her eyes. *No,* he says. *It's not. But it's a start.*

*Emotional purpose: The deepest Thomas-Eleanor scene so far. Physical proximity, emotional honesty, mutual recognition.*
*Tension level: 4/5*
*Ending hook: She leaves her hand on his arm for a moment when she thanks him. He watches her walk back to the house and does not move for a while.*

---

**CHAPTER 18: "The First Choice"**
*POV: Cecile (close third, moving into shared-scene)*

Cecile finds Eleanor in the upstairs bathroom, crying for the first time. Not polite funeral tears — actual grief, bent over the sink. Cecile goes in without asking. She runs the water and guides Eleanor's face into it and then wraps her in the big towel and sits with her on the bathroom floor until it's done.

Afterward, Eleanor asks Cecile to stay. They move to the bedroom — Eleanor's first time back in the bed — and Cecile sits against the headboard and Eleanor lies with her head near Cecile's shoulder and they talk for a long time about grief and desire and guilt, and Cecile is honest about her own feelings, and Eleanor listens. When Eleanor finally asks if Cecile wants this — wants her — Cecile says yes, plainly, without strategy.

Eleanor says: *I'm not ready tonight. But I want to know that it's there.* Cecile says: *It's there. It will stay there until you want it or until you don't, and either way I'll still be here.*

*Emotional purpose: The first fully consensual, explicit acknowledgment of mutual desire. Eleanor asserts her timeline. The emotional breakthrough of Act Two.*
*Tension level: 4/5*
*Ending hook: Eleanor sleeps in her own bed for the first time since the funeral. In the morning, she opens the fifth letter.*

---

### ACT THREE: THE CHOICE IS HERS (Chapters 19–24)
*The final letters. The video. The widow decides.*

---

**CHAPTER 19: "Letters Five and Six"**
*POV: Eleanor (first person)*

Eleanor reads letters five and six in one sitting. Letter five is a love letter — specific, private, about their early years, the details of his love for her body and her mind, written as though he knows he is running out of chances to say it. It is devastating and tender. Eleanor cries through it.

Letter six is an apology, written differently — more formal, as if he's choosing words carefully. He writes about the instructions he gave the others. He says: *I want to be clear, Ellie: these people were not given permission to approach you. They were given permission to be present. The difference is important to me, and I hope it is to you. Nothing in any letter I wrote to them changes the fact that you are the only person in this story with any real authority. If you want them near you, they are yours to choose. If you want none of this, burn every envelope. I'll understand. I've always understood you better than you think.*

*Emotional purpose: Final clarification of the moral framework. Marcus at his clearest and most human.*
*Tension level: 2/5*
*Ending hook: One letter left. And the USB drive.*

**CHAPTER 20: "The Things She Chooses"**
*POV: Eleanor (first person)*

This chapter spans several evenings and mornings. Eleanor and Cecile, together at last, with gentleness and honest desire and the specific tenderness that Cecile has been holding in reserve. This is the first fully intimate scene of the novel — emotional, physical, driven by Eleanor's choice and her words. Eleanor is present, curious, and unashamed.

The scene is written with restraint and heat in equal measure: the candles, the old house holding its breath around them, the way intimacy can feel like both arrival and discovery. Cecile is attentive. Eleanor is the one who chooses the pace.

Afterward, Eleanor lies in the dark and is aware she feels no guilt. She expected guilt. She waits for it. It does not come.

*Emotional purpose: Eleanor's first chosen intimacy after Marcus's death. The focus is on her agency, her presence, and the absence of shame.*
*Tension level: 5/5*
*Ending hook: "He planned this," she says to the ceiling. "I know," Cecile says. "Does that bother you?" Eleanor considers. "Ask me in the morning."*

**CHAPTER 21: "The Weight of a Good Man"**
*POV: Julian (close third)*

Julian comes to the estate and Eleanor meets him in the garden. They have not spoken properly since the night of the storm. She is different now — more present, warmer, unselfconscious in a way she hasn't been. Julian notices all of it and says nothing. He is aware that something has shifted and that he is both glad of it and in a kind of pain.

They walk the grounds together. At the garden wall, Eleanor stops and faces him. She says: *Tell me what the letter actually said. Yours. Word for word.* Julian says he can bring it to her. She says she doesn't need the paper. She says: *Tell me.* He does. Standing against the stone wall with winter light on his face, Julian tells her everything Marcus wrote.

When he is done, Eleanor is quiet for a long time. Then she steps forward and puts her hand flat against his chest, over his heart — not a gesture of romance, but of contact. A claim. *He knew,* she says. *He knew everything.* Julian says: *Yes.* She says: *Did he know you'd fall in love with me or did he arrange it?* Julian says: *Both, I think. That's the part I can't forgive him for.* Eleanor says: *I know.*

*Emotional purpose: Julian and Eleanor's most honest scene. The complexity of love between the living in the shadow of a man who arranged it.*
*Tension level: 4/5*
*Ending hook: She doesn't move her hand. He doesn't move either. Then Thomas calls from the far end of the garden and the moment passes, and they walk back, and they are both aware that something is beginning.*

**CHAPTER 22: "What the Garden Holds"**
*POV: Thomas (close third)*

The chapter of Thomas and Eleanor, alone in the greenhouse as the days lengthen. He tells her, with the same economy he applies to everything, that he has been in love with her since her second winter at the estate. He says it like reporting weather. He is not performing. Eleanor looks at him for a long time and then asks why he never said anything. He says: *Because you were married.* She says: *And now?* He says: *Now you're not.*

What follows is the Thomas-Eleanor scene: slow, physical, present-tense, built from two people who have been paying close attention to each other for a long time and no longer have any reason not to say so. The greenhouse is warm and glassed and full of green growing things. Eleanor has chosen this. She says so.

*Emotional purpose: The Thomas arc completes. Physical intimacy as safety and rootedness.*
*Tension level: 5/5*
*Ending hook: Afterward, Eleanor sits in the warm greenhouse air and thinks: Marcus wanted this. And also: Marcus doesn't get credit for this. This was mine.*

**CHAPTER 23: "The Final Video"**
*POV: Eleanor (first person)*

Eleanor opens the USB drive. There is a single file: a video. She watches it alone in the library, on Marcus's laptop, in the chair he used to sit in.

Marcus is on screen in his office. He looks healthy, alive, achingly familiar. He speaks to her for forty-one minutes. He tells her everything the letters couldn't carry — his voice, his face, the way he looked when he said *I love you* and meant it entirely. He talks about Julian, about Cecile, about Thomas. He says: *I know this might feel like manipulation. I've thought about that. I think the difference between manipulation and love is whether the person being cared for retains their freedom. I've tried to preserve yours. I hope I managed it.* He pauses. He says: *I also want to tell you that I knew, the whole time, that I wasn't enough. Not completely. That's a terrible thing to know about yourself and I wasn't brave enough to say it out loud when I could. I'm saying it now.*

He signs off: *Take care of yourself, Ellie. You don't need them to do it. But you don't have to do it alone either. Those are different things. I learned that too late.* The screen goes dark.

*Emotional purpose: The novel's emotional center of gravity. Marcus at his most honest and most complicated. Eleanor receives the full accounting.*
*Tension level: 3/5*
*Ending hook: She closes the laptop. She sits with the silence. Then she opens the seventh and final envelope.*

**CHAPTER 24: "Her Choice"**
*POV: Eleanor (first person)*

The final letter is short. Three sentences: *The instructions I gave them were real, but they were also a gift for me — the thing I couldn't do myself. I'm asking you to forgive me for that. Whatever you choose from here: it's yours, Ellie. All of it. Yours.*

Eleanor reads it once. She folds it. She puts it in the box with the others.

Then she does something Marcus's letters could never anticipate: she calls Cecile upstairs and Thomas in from the garden and Julian from the city, and when they are all assembled in the library — the four of them, the house around them, the winter light coming through the tall windows — she tells them she has read everything. She tells them she knows what was asked of them. She thanks them, with the specific courtesy of someone who means it.

Then she says: *None of you are here because of his letters anymore. You're here because I'm asking you to be. That's the only instruction that matters now.* She meets each of their eyes in turn: Cecile, Thomas, Julian. *Can you understand the difference?* All three of them can.

The novel ends with Eleanor at the window. The garden below is beginning to show the first green of late winter. She holds the seven folded letters in one hand and the USB drive in the other. She is thirty-one years old and her husband is dead and she is standing in a house full of people who love her, and she chose every one of them.

*She sets the envelopes on the mantel, between two white candles. She does not burn them. They were, after all, written with love.*

*Emotional purpose: Full resolution of the central moral question. Eleanor's agency is total. The ending is warm, open, and genuinely earned.*
*Tension level: 3/5*
*Ending hook (series hook): Three months later, a package arrives from Marcus's attorney. Inside: a second set of sealed envelopes. The addresses are for cities she has never been to. People she has never met. The instructions on the front read: "For Eleanor."*

## PART FOUR: RECURRING MOTIFS AND SYMBOLS

**Sealed Envelopes**
The central object of the novel. Sealed knowledge. The tension between what is kept from Eleanor and what she is owed. As she opens each letter, a seal breaks — both literally and psychologically.

**Locked Rooms/Cabinets**
Marcus's locked cabinet. The library he keeps in private order. Locked spaces in the novel represent the interior life — what is protected, what is hidden, what must be chosen to be opened.

**Rain and Glass**
Rain on windows recurs in every major emotional scene. Glass (the greenhouse, the conservatory, the storm windows) creates transparency with barrier — closeness without touch, visibility without access. This imagery maps directly onto the novel's central tension.

**Handwriting**
Marcus's handwriting is described consistently — his specific letterforms, the way his pen pressed into paper. Eleanor learns to read his emotional state through the variation in his script. Handwriting in this novel is intimacy on the page.

**Candlelight vs. Electric Light**
Candlelight scenes are intimate, soft, chosen. Electric light is stark, re-grounding, occasionally harsh. The storm chapter uses this duality explicitly. The return of the lights after the storm represents a kind of reality intrusion.

**The Garden / Earth**
Thomas's domain. The garden is alive, cyclical, present-tense. In contrast to the house (memory, stasis, Marcus) and the library (words, grief, the past), the garden insists on now. Working with soil is Eleanor's first genuine return to her body.

**Perfume**
Marcus's scent (sandalwood and cedar) fades slowly from his personal spaces across the novel. It is a kind of grief-marking — Eleanor notices each time it weakens. When it is finally gone, she is ready.

**White Wine / Red Wine**
White wine: the earlier scenes, colder, more guarded. Red wine: warmth, intimacy, the later chapters. The color of what is in the glass marks where Eleanor is emotionally.

**The Widow's Window Seat**
Eleanor returns to the same window seat throughout the novel. It is her observation point, her grief post, her threshold. Over the course of the novel, she moves away from it — and the final chapter finds her at the window, but standing.

**The Number Seven**
Seven envelopes. Seven chapters in Act One. Seven years Cecile has worked the estate. Marcus's specific attention to the number is noted by Eleanor; whether it is coincidence or design is left open.

## PART SIX: BACK-COVER BLURB

---

*The first letter arrived three days after the funeral. Not to me. To them.*

Eleanor Voss is living in the aftermath of a marriage — in the sprawling estate her husband left her, surrounded by the people he trusted most: Cecile, who knows every secret the house has ever kept. Thomas, who tends the grounds with a silence that says more than most men manage with words. And Julian, Marcus's closest friend, who has been calling every evening at eight o'clock, like a promise he made to himself before he knew what it would cost.

They all received letters. In her husband's handwriting. With the same instruction on the front.

*Take Care of Her.*

Eleanor assumes the letters are practical. Finances. Security. The basic architecture of surviving loss. She is wrong — and right — in ways she will spend the rest of winter learning.

Because Marcus Voss loved his wife the way some people love rare, irreplaceable things: absolutely, obsessively, and with a thoroughness that reached past his death. He knew what she needed. He knew what she hadn't admitted to herself. He knew the specific shape of her loneliness inside a marriage she had, in many ways, loved.

And so he planned.

But Eleanor is not a woman who follows instructions she didn't write. And grief, it turns out, has its own ideas about what comes next.

*Dark, sensuous, and emotionally relentless,* **Take Care of Her** *is a gothic estate romance about the complicated geometry of love and control, desire and guilt, and the question that haunts every beautiful arrangement:*

*When someone plans your healing — is the choice still yours?*

# TAKE CARE OF HER

## Chapter One: Three Days After

The first letter arrived three days after the funeral. Not to me. To them.

I was at the library window when the courier came up the drive — a young man in a gray jacket, precise in the way of people who deliver things they aren't allowed to know. I watched him hand Cecile an envelope at the front door and then walk back to his car and drive away. Cecile stood in the doorway for a moment with the envelope in both hands, and I noticed she didn't open it. She just stood there, in the gray October morning, holding it like it was something that needed to be held carefully.

I went back to my book. I wasn't reading it.

Thirty minutes later I saw the car on the service road along the south garden. Another courier — different car, different jacket — and this one found Thomas at the back gate. I watched Thomas take the envelope and tuck it inside his work jacket without looking at it. He went back to the beds he'd been tending, and I watched the way he moved for a while after that. Slower. Deliberate. Like a man rearranging something inside himself.

Julian's envelope reached him at his flat in the city. I didn't see that one arrive. But he called at eight o'clock that night — as he had the two nights before, and the night before that — and his voice was different. Still careful. But carrying something new underneath it, the way water sounds different when the depth changes.

I didn't ask.

He talked about a documentary he'd been watching. A restaurant that had closed on the corner near his office. A story about Marcus from their second year at university that I'd heard a version of before, but not this version, not with these particular details that I filed away automatically, the way I have always filed Marcus away — in the particular drawers of memory organized by context: Marcus in his thirties, Marcus laughing, Marcus as someone else knew him.

"I'll call tomorrow," Julian said.

"I know," I said.

That was three days after the funeral. This is where the story starts, though I didn't know it then. I thought the story was already over. I thought what I was living in was the aftermath — the long, featureless plain of after, where everything would be about managing what Marcus had left rather than anything being about me.

I was wrong about that. Marcus had been extremely careful to make sure I was wrong about that.

---

The house at that hour was the specific kind of quiet that old houses achieve when they've decided to mourn alongside you. I'd grown up in apartments — stacked, thin-walled, cheerfully indifferent to the lives inside them — and married into stone and forty-seven rooms and grounds that required their own management staff. I had learned to love the house. Its weight. The particular sound of its silences, which varied by room and hour and weather in a way I still, after five years, found interesting.

I was sleeping in the library.

I told myself it was because the bed was too large. Marcus had been a man who filled space — not loudly, not obtrusively, but *thoroughly*, the way certain plants claim the soil around them. The bed without him wasn't empty so much as it was wrong in its proportions, like a word mispronounced.

The library, by contrast, still held him in a way I could manage in small doses. His books, organized in the system no one else had ever understood. His pen caddy on the desk, which I had not touched. The chair by the fireplace where he used to sit at seven in the morning with his coffee and read the news without speaking, and I would come down and sit on the sofa across from him and we would exist companionably in the same room without needing to perform it.

I missed that. I missed it more than I had expected to, and what I had expected was to be destroyed, so the fact that I was merely in pain and not destroyed felt like its own kind of bewilderment.

I wasn't crying. I want to be clear about that. At the funeral, in the days after, in the long evenings when the house breathed around me and Cecile left tea trays I didn't touch — I was not crying. There was something wrong with that, I thought. There was something wrong with me, the way grief had decided to arrive not as collapse but as a very precise, very thorough numbness, as if I had been vacuum-sealed.

I had read that this was normal. I had read a number of things, in the days after, because reading is what I do when I need to feel that I am doing something.

---

The morning of the letters, I came downstairs at six forty-five and found Cecile already in the kitchen, standing at the window with a cup of coffee, looking at the garden. She turned when she heard me and her expression did something careful and specific.

"You were up early," I said.

"Couldn't sleep," she said.

This was unusual. Cecile is a woman of such thorough competence that she seems to have solved for most human difficulties, including sleeplessness. I noted it the way I note most things — with the external precision that is, I have come to understand, my primary emotional mechanism. I observe. I catalog. I defer feeling until I have a complete inventory.

"There's coffee," Cecile said.

"I see it," I said.

I poured a cup and stood beside her at the window. The south garden was silver with frost in the early light. Thomas was already there — he is always already there, at any hour I happen to look — moving along the hedgerow with his slow, purposeful gait, breath misting in the cold. He was wearing the green wax jacket he wears from October through March. He did not look up at the window, which is not unusual for Thomas. Thomas is a man engaged with the ground immediately in front of him, in the excellent way of people who have decided that the world directly available to them is enough.

"How are you this morning?" Cecile asked.

"The same," I said.

She nodded. She understood this as the answer it was: not a deflection, but a report. The same as yesterday. The same as the day before. Whatever the same is.

I watched Thomas make his slow passage along the far hedgerow, and then I watched the frost on the garden, and then I watched my own hands around the coffee cup — the ring still on my finger, which I had not thought consciously about removing or keeping, and the question of which I would do when had not yet arrived.

"A courier came yesterday," I said.

I had not intended to say this. Cecile was quiet for a moment.

"Yes," she said.

"For you."

"Yes."

I looked at her. Cecile met my eyes with the specific quality of attention she deploys when she is telling the truth and would prefer not to tell more of it.

"What was in it?" I asked.

"Instructions," she said. "From Marcus."

I looked back at the garden.

"About the house?" I said.

"About you," Cecile said.

The word landed differently than I'd expected. *About you.* Not *about the estate, about the finances, about the staff schedule, about the maintenance of the grounds through the winter months.* About me. Marcus, three days dead, had arranged for a courier to bring Cecile instructions about me.

This was, I thought, almost funny. Almost. Marcus had always believed in preparation. He had an emergency protocol for weather events, for power failure, for every variety of household disruption. He had updated our wills two months before he died, which I had not registered as ominous until after, at which point it had become the particular kind of detail I was unable to stop thinking about.

Of course he had arranged something. Of course.

"Thomas also received one," I said.

"Yes."

"And Julian."

A pause. "Yes."

I finished my coffee. I set the cup in the sink. I looked at my hands again — the ring, the knuckles, the small scar on my left index finger from a kitchen accident in our first year, which Marcus had made a dramatic production of and I had found annoying and tender in equal measure.

"When you say instructions," I said, carefully, "do you mean practical things? The house, the finances—"

"Some of that," Cecile said. "And some other things."

"What other things?"

She looked at me for a long moment. Cecile has a way of looking at a person that suggests she is calculating not what to say but how much to say, and specifically whether the person in front of her is ready for the quantity she has available.

"Come to dinner tonight," she said. "I'll make something proper. And I'll tell you what I can."

"What you can," I repeated.

"Some of it is private," she said. "Some of it is yours."

Outside, Thomas had stopped working and was standing with his face turned toward the house. Not looking at the window — or if he was, I couldn't tell. He stood still for a moment in the silver morning light, and then he bent back to the ground.

I thought about the letter. About what it might say. About what it would mean that Marcus — meticulous, thorough, loving, impossible Marcus — had written it and sent it to the people closest to my life.

*About you,* she'd said.

"All right," I said. "Dinner."

I went back upstairs to the library. I did not read. I looked out the window at the garden for a long time, watching Thomas work, and thought about all the things Marcus might have known about me that I had not yet had the courage to know myself.

He had always been better at that than I was.

*End of Chapter One*
