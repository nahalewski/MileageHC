# TAKE CARE OF HER
## Chapter Three: Saturday

---

Julian arrived at two o'clock in the afternoon, exactly when he'd said he would, because Julian was the kind of man who arrived exactly when he said he would. I heard the car on the drive and stayed in the library and did not go down to the door, which Cecile answered. I listened to the sound of his voice in the entry hall — low, brief, the register of it familiar — and I heard Cecile take his coat and say something I couldn't catch, and then his footsteps on the stairs.

He knocked on the library door even though it was open.

"Come in," I said, which was unnecessary, and we both knew it.

He looked the same as he always looked. Dark coat off now, grey wool over a white shirt, the sleeves rolled back once in the way he'd always done and that Marcus had teased him about at every autumn dinner for five years, something about a man who couldn't commit to either formal or informal and had chosen instead to be a walking argument about it. He had dark circles, which I hadn't seen through a phone call. His hair was slightly disorganized in the way it got when he'd run a hand through it once too often, which he did when he was working through something.

I had not looked at Julian the way I was looking at him right now in a long time. Possibly ever. I had looked at Julian the way you look at the furniture of your closest friendship with your husband — familiarly, without examination, the way you look at things that are fixtures of the room. I looked at him now and noticed several things I had filed without opening.

He was looking at me differently.

That was the first thing. He was looking at me the way he sometimes looked at things he was deciding about — fully, without the polite deflection he usually applied to the space between us. He caught me noticing and didn't look away.

"You look tired," he said.

"So do you," I said.

He sat in the chair across from the sofa — Marcus's chair, which no one had sat in since, and which Julian sat in now with the specific unselfconsciousness of someone who had decided to stop being careful about certain things. I watched him do it. I watched him put his hands on the arms of the chair and settle into it and look at me from it without apology, and something in the room adjusted its temperature by a degree.

"You read your letter," I said.

"Yes."

"And you've decided not to tell me what it says."

He was quiet for a moment. "Cecile told you there were letters."

"She told me the shape of things. Not the content."

"The content is mine until it isn't," he said. "Until you want to have that conversation."

I looked at him. He looked back. The fire was low and the afternoon light was thin and gray through the casement windows and the room smelled of old paper and cedar, which was the smell of Marcus's books, which was the smell of this room on any given Saturday when I had brought tea and sat in the same position I was in now while Marcus read and Julian drank his whisky and talked about whatever needed talking about.

It was the same room. He was sitting in the same chair. The only thing that had changed was the quality of how he was looking at me, and the absence of the person who used to be in this room with us, and I was aware of both of those things with the same intensity I was aware of the fire going low, which was: immediately, entirely, throughout my body.

"I don't want that conversation today," I said.

"All right."

"I want a drink and company and for someone to be in this room with me who doesn't look at me like I might break."

Julian reached for the whisky on the side table — he'd brought it up himself, or Cecile had put it there — and he poured two glasses without asking if I wanted one. He passed mine across the low table and looked at me with the specific expression of someone who is not looking at you like you might break, and I felt the particular relief of that like a shift in pressure.

---

We talked for three hours.

This was not unusual — Julian and I had always talked with ease, had always had the particular conversational fluency that comes from years of shared reference, shared history, the same people and the same table and the same accumulated understanding of how things work. We had talked for three hours before. But it was different now in a way I was tracking without directly examining, the way you track something in your peripheral vision that you're not ready to look at directly.

He sat in Marcus's chair and he didn't lean away from me the way he usually did. Julian had always had a quality of slight remove — a courtesy distance he maintained, a physical polite that I had registered without thinking about it the way you register the etiquette of a room. Today that quality was different. Not gone. But different, the way a door that was closed is different when it is only mostly closed, when there is a sliver of light and air between it and the frame.

He laughed at something I said about the estate attorney — a genuine laugh, mouth wide, head back, and it changed his whole face, and I found myself looking at the changed face longer than I needed to.

He refilled my glass without asking.

When the afternoon light shifted to evening and the fire needed tending, he got up and put wood on it without asking about that either, crouching by the grate with his back to me, and I looked at the set of his shoulders in the grey wool and at his hands on the iron and then I looked away, out the window at the dark garden, and I thought about Marcus with a specific jolt — his absence not as absence but as presence, the sensation of him behind me in the room, and I took a breath and set my glass down.

"Stay for dinner," I said when Julian stood up.

He turned around. Something in his expression when he looked at me now was different from everything that had come before it in five years. It was not dramatic. It was not performed. It was simply the face of a man who had stopped rearranging himself for the benefit of a situation that no longer existed.

"All right," he said.

---

Cecile made something with lamb and herbs from the winter garden and left it with wine on the dining room table and then, with the specific tact of a woman who understands what a room needs, took herself upstairs.

Julian and I sat across from each other at the long dining table, which was too big for two people and had always been too big for two people, and we ate and talked and the candles burned down and I poured more wine without thinking about it and he talked about Marcus — for the first time directly, with something that was not just grief but memory, the specific laughter-tinged grief of remembering someone you loved who was also absurd and particular and completely themselves. He told me about a trip they'd taken at twenty-five, a terrible camping experiment that Marcus had organized with complete confidence and that had gone comprehensively wrong in ways that were, in Julian's telling, almost operatic. I laughed until my eyes were wet and I couldn't tell at the time whether all of it was laughter.

Afterward we took our wine back to the library.

We sat on the sofa. Not on opposite ends of it, the way we'd always arranged ourselves — I sat where I always sat and Julian sat closer than he'd ever sat, the space between us reduced from the careful foot and a half to something that was almost nothing, close enough that when he turned his head I could see the candlelight moving in his eyes.

He was not doing anything. He was sitting close and talking about nothing in particular and not doing anything at all. But the proximity was itself a thing and we were both aware of it and neither of us moved away from it.

At some point the talking stopped.

I'm not sure when, exactly. We'd been talking and then we weren't, and the fire was low again and the room was quiet except for the old house shifting around us, its particular dark-winter sounds — timber settling, a window's draft, the silence that old stone makes when it has held people for a long time. Julian was looking at me the way he'd been looking at me all day but now there was nothing else happening in the room to distribute the attention and so the look was simply there, direct and unmediated, and I looked back.

His hand moved first.

Not a dramatic gesture. Not a reaching or a grasping. He simply set his hand on the sofa between us, palm up, open — not touching me, not demanding, just: available. The way you leave a door open. I looked at his hand and then at his face and the expression there was not hungry, not urgent. It was careful and honest and it had been waiting for five years.

I put my hand in his.

---

I want to be precise about what happened next because I have thought about it many times since and the precision matters to me.

He looked at our hands together for a moment and then at my face, and I looked back, and I was aware of the specific and terrible fact that I wanted him — not as comfort, not as replacement, not as any of the sensible explanations for what was happening in my chest. I wanted Julian Ashworth with a specificity and a clarity that had apparently been living somewhere in me for long enough that its arrival now felt less like a surprise than a recognition. Like a word I hadn't known I knew.

He lifted my hand and held it for a moment between both of his, the careful warmth of it, and then he leaned forward and kissed me.

Softly, at first — the tentative quality of something beginning, a question more than a statement. I answered it. I leaned in and I kissed him back and I felt the precise warm shock of it move through me, the sensation of my own want suddenly given a direction, and his hand came to my face — fingertips at my jaw, the lightest possible weight, as though he was afraid I'd pull back and wanted to be ready to let me.

I did not pull back. Not then.

His thumb moved along my cheekbone. I felt his exhale against my mouth and I felt the quality of his restraint — a thing held in careful check, wanting more than it was taking — and I moved closer to him and felt him respond, his hand at my waist now, his arm around me, drawing me toward him with a gentleness that felt, somehow, like the thing I'd been most in need of without knowing I'd been in need of anything at all.

We had moved without my registering the transition from sitting upright to the sofa's length, from two separate people to something more tangled — his body warm against mine, the smell of him close in my face, the specific human warmth of a man who has wanted to be here for a long time and cannot quite believe he is. I had both hands in his hair and his mouth was at my throat and I was in my body in a way I had not been in my body for weeks, the long numbness cracking open under the pressure of simple contact, and I was grateful and desperate and—

Marcus.

Not a thought. A sensation. His smell — cedar and sandalwood, the exact compound of him that had been fading slowly from the bedroom I wouldn't sleep in, and now for a moment it was here, in this room, and I didn't know if it was real or my mind's invention but it stopped me as completely as a hand on my shoulder.

I went still.

Julian felt it immediately. He lifted his head and looked at me without pulling away.

"Ellie."

I was looking at the fireplace. At the chair across from us — Marcus's chair, which Julian had sat in today, and in which Marcus had sat on every other Saturday I could remember, reading with one leg crossed and his glasses on, occasionally remarking on whatever he was reading. I was looking at that chair and feeling the specific and terrible quality of absence, which is not nothing — it is something, a shaped nothing, a space that holds the exact dimension of what used to be in it.

"I'm sorry," I said.

"Don't be." Julian's hand was still at my back, very still, not demanding. "We don't have to—"

"It's not that I don't want to." I looked at him. His expression was open and careful and not wounded, which was — he was not making this about himself, he was entirely present for whatever I needed, and I knew this was Julian Ashworth's specific quality of love and had known it for years and had filed it. "I want to. That's the problem."

He was quiet, waiting.

"I want you," I said, plainly, because plainness felt like the only thing I had the energy for. "And wanting you feels like something I should be ashamed of. It's been three days."

"It's been seventeen days," Julian said. "Since Stockholm."

"You know what I mean."

"I know what you mean." He said it gently, not correcting me, not arguing. "And I can't tell you how to feel about it. But I can tell you that sitting here with you is not something I did to make you feel guilty."

"I know that."

"And I can tell you—" He stopped. He said the next thing slowly, like someone stepping onto uncertain ground. "I can tell you that Marcus knew. About how I feel about you. He said so in the letter. He asked me, specifically, not to — not to hide it from you anymore. When you were ready."

I looked at him.

"He asked you," I said.

"Yes."

"He planned this."

Julian considered the word. "He made room for a possibility. There's a difference." He paused. "Though I'll grant the difference might not be very large."

I looked at the chair again. The empty chair. The specific shape of what was not in it.

Here is what I knew, sitting with Julian's arm still around me and my heart doing something complicated: Marcus had loved me. He had also, in the specific way of his loving, always arranged the room before I arrived in it. He had curated the conditions of things my entire marriage and it had felt like care and it had sometimes been suffocating and now, three weeks after his death, he was apparently still doing it — still managing, still arranging, still placing the people in the positions he'd chosen with the faith that his design was sufficient.

Here is what else I knew: I was sitting in a firelit library at ten o'clock at night with a man I wanted, and my husband was dead, and I had been running on nothing but numbness and managed grief for seventeen days, and I could feel — under all of it, under the guilt and the grief and the particular shame of wanting anything at all in this season of loss — I could feel that I was alive.

My body was alive. Urgently, stubbornly, irreducibly alive, regardless of whether I had decided to let it be.

I looked at Julian.

He was watching me with the patient attention of a man who has decided the outcome is not his to determine. Not performing patience — actually patient, the kind that comes from having already made his peace with whatever I chose. He would leave if I asked him to. He would stay. He would sit in silence until I was ready to speak. He was entirely, without demand, available.

I kissed him.

Not the tentative first kiss. Something else — something that came from the honest center of what I'd been feeling, direct and unhesitating, and I felt him catch his breath against my mouth before he kissed me back, and when he kissed me back it was different, the restraint pulled thinner now, the warmth behind it more present, and his hand came to my hair.

"Ellie." My name. Just that.

"I know," I said. "I know what I'm doing."

And I did. This is the thing I need to say clearly, now, looking back: I knew what I was doing. It was not recklessness and it was not numbing myself and it was not Marcus's arrangement making the choice for me. It was me, in my own body, making a deliberate and clear-eyed decision to let myself want something, because I was alive and I was tired of performing the death of my own hunger out of some obligation to an appropriate grief timeline that no one had written down anywhere and that had no authority over what I needed.

I wanted this. I chose it. Those two things were mine, regardless of what any letter said.

---

I pulled him toward me.

He came willingly, without hesitation — the five-year careful distance finally gone, and what replaced it was warmth, real and immediate, the warmth of a body that had been waiting a long time to stop being disciplined. His hands moved over me with an attention that made me feel exactly the thing I had not known I'd been starving for: like I was being studied, not managed. Like he was paying the kind of attention that asks rather than assumes.

He kissed my throat again and I let my head fall back against the sofa cushion and I felt the sensation move through me with a clarity that was almost shocking — the specific aliveness of being touched by someone who was entirely present, whose attention was entirely on me, who was not thinking about anything else in the world.

"You're shaking," he said, against my collarbone.

"I know," I said.

He lifted his head and looked at me. "Tell me if—"

"I'll tell you," I said. "I will. Keep going."

He kept his eyes on my face for one moment more — checking, not performing the check, actually checking — and then he kissed me again and I wrapped my hand in his hair and pulled him closer and stopped performing the version of myself that didn't want things.

The firelight moved on the walls. The old house held its breath around us. Outside, the winter garden was dark and still and the stars had come out after the rain, and in the library with its books and its candles and the smell of cedar and old paper and the warmth of Julian's hands moving across my back I was not a widow. I was not a woman in the carefully managed aftermath of a loss. I was not numb and I was not performing composure and I was not being appropriate.

I was just a woman who wanted something and was taking it.

There was a moment — near the end, when the fire was low and my breath was gone and his face was very close to mine and he was looking at me with an expression I had never seen him wear because he had never been allowed to wear it — when I thought about Marcus not with grief and not with guilt but with a strange clear gratitude. Not for the letter. Not for the arrangement. For the fifteen years before the arrangement, the fifteen years of friendship with this man, the friendship that had made Julian the specific person he was: someone who had learned patience, someone who had learned to be present without possessing, someone who understood the difference between a gift offered and a gift taken.

I had chosen a gift that had been offered freely.

When it was over I lay in the candle's last light with my head against Julian's chest and listened to his breathing slow and thought about the word *alive* — how it applies to the body in particular and involuntary ways, how it is not a state you can argue with no matter how much grief tells you it would be more appropriate to be otherwise.

Julian's hand moved slowly up my back, a long unhurried stroke from hip to shoulder, thoughtful.

"All right?" he said.

"Yes," I said. And then, because it was true and because truth had decided tonight was its night: "More than all right."

He held me for a while without speaking. The fire died to coals. The house settled around us into its deep winter silence — stone and timber, old and patient, holding us the way houses hold people who've lived in them a long time. I could hear the wind at the east windows. I could smell the garden.

I did not cry. I had been waiting since the funeral to cry and it still had not come and I wondered if this was why — if the feeling I'd been saving for grief had found a different shape tonight and spent itself that way instead. Not grief but its shadow. Not loss but the particular rawness of its opposite.

I was still thinking about Marcus.

Not in the way I was afraid I'd think about him — not as an accusation, not as a presence at my shoulder with an expression of betrayal. He was in this room the way he was always in this room: in the books, in the cedar, in the chair across from us, in the specific quality of the lamplight that he had chosen when we furnished the house together in the first year of the marriage. He was in the house the way people persist in houses that have held them. That was allowed. That was, in fact, right.

I was allowed to have this thing. And he was allowed to be here. Both of those were true at the same time.

I had thought they were incompatible. I had assumed, somewhere in the seventeen days of numbness, that wanting anything again would require the evacuation of everything Marcus had been and meant. That the living and the dead could not coexist in the same room.

The room contradicted me.

"I need to tell you something," Julian said.

"Mm."

"I've been in love with you for five years."

I was quiet for a moment. "I know."

"I wasn't going to say anything. Before the letter, I mean. I had — it was managed. I'd managed it."

"I know," I said again.

He paused. "How long have you known?"

I thought about it honestly. "About two years," I said. "Maybe three. I didn't — I didn't let myself examine it directly. I filed it in a drawer."

"What kind of drawer?"

"The kind I don't open unless I have to."

He was quiet. "Are you opening it now?"

I thought about the word *now.* The specificity of now — this room, this evening, the coals in the grate, the feeling of being inside my own body for the first time in weeks. The question was not whether the drawer was open. The drawer was off its hinges. The question was what I was going to do with what was in it.

"I don't know what I'm doing," I said honestly. "I know what I did tonight and I know I chose it and I don't regret it. Beyond that I don't have much architecture yet."

"That's all right," Julian said. "I'm not asking for architecture."

"What are you asking for?"

He was quiet for a long moment. Then: "Nothing, right now. I'm asking for nothing right now. I'm just — here. That's all."

I lay in the candlelight and listened to his breathing and thought that *here* was more than I'd had in seventeen days and more than I'd asked for and possibly, quietly, exactly what I needed.

Outside, the first snow of the season had started — I could hear it, the specific silence that snow creates, the muffled quality of the world when it is covering itself in white. I thought about the garden under it. Thomas's winter beds. The greenhouse holding its amber warmth.

The house around me. The three lit windows.

The drawer, off its hinges, full of things I would have to look at eventually.

Not tonight. Tonight I was warm, and the room smelled of cedar, and Julian's hand was on my back, and I was unreservedly alive.

That was enough for now. That was, tonight, everything.

---

*End of Chapter Three*
