# TAKE CARE OF HER
## Chapter Two: What the Letters Say

---

Julian Ashworth received his envelope at eight forty-three in the morning, three days after the funeral and twelve days after the phone call from Stockholm, and he would later think of it as the moment his life divided into before and after — not Marcus's death, which had been the obvious dividing line — but this. The envelope. The handwriting he recognized before he registered that he was recognizing it.

He'd been at the kitchen table since six with a coffee gone cold beside him and the kind of sleeplessness that accumulates into a physical texture, the flat tiredness of a man whose body won't rest because his mind won't let it. He heard the post come through the slot. He didn't move.

He let it sit on the doormat for eleven minutes.

When he finally crossed to the door, the envelope was the first thing he saw — cream vellum, heavy stock, face-up on the mat amid the ordinary post. Marcus's handwriting. Decisive, forward-leaning, the lowercase letters a little large, the capitals restrained. Julian had read that handwriting on birthday cards and legal documents and the note Marcus left on his desk the morning of Eleanor's surprise thirtieth birthday party that said only: *Don't be late. She'll know if you're late.* He had not seen it in fourteen days because Marcus Voss was dead.

Below Julian's name and address, in the same decisive hand:

*Take Care of Her.*

He took the envelope to the kitchen table. He sat with it in front of him and looked at it. The wax seal was dark burgundy, Marcus's initials pressed into it — the kind of thing Marcus did when he wanted to signal: *this is not ordinary. This is the formal version. Pay attention.*

Julian broke the seal.

---

Three pages. He read the first two lines and set the letter face-down on the table and stared at the wall.

Then he picked it up again, because putting it down didn't change what was in it.

He had a method for reading significant things — he'd had it since university, when Marcus had first observed it and said: *you read like a lawyer. Like every sentence is evidence.* Julian had pointed out that he was, in fact, a lawyer. Marcus had said that wasn't the point. The method was: once quickly, to understand the shape of it, and once slowly, to let each part arrive fully. He applied it now. The first reading took four minutes and left him standing at the window watching the street without knowing how he'd gotten there. He went back to the table for the second reading and read every word the way Marcus had written them — with intention, with weight, with the particular authority of a man who knew he wasn't going to be available for follow-up questions.

---

The letter began with the friendship. Fifteen years of it, named specifically — *the flat in the city in your thirties, the summer in Lisbon, the week we didn't speak after the Hargreaves deal and the week after that when you came over and neither of us mentioned it and that was how it was resolved.* Marcus wrote about friendship the way he wrote about everything: precisely, without sentimentality, but with an underlying warmth that he trusted the precision to carry. He wrote: *You have been the best and most honest friend I have ever had. I want you to know that I know that. I want you to know that whatever I'm about to ask you does not come from a place of using that friendship. It comes from trusting it with the most important thing I have.*

Julian set the letter down at this point and breathed carefully for a moment.

He picked it up again.

*The most important thing I have* was Eleanor. He knew this before he reached it. Marcus continued:

*She was lonely in our marriage. I want to write that plainly because I spent too much time not saying it plainly when I was alive. I loved her completely and I was, in specific ways that I understood better in the last year than I ever had before, not enough for her. I held her too tightly in some ways and not closely enough in others. I was good at possession and not always good at presence. Eleanor needed a man who would look at her like she was the whole room, and I looked at her like she was mine — which is not the same thing, and the difference mattered to her even when she didn't say so, and she usually didn't say so because she was protecting me from the knowledge that I was failing her in that particular direction.*

Julian read this passage twice. He thought about the dinners he'd attended at the Voss house over five years, the way Eleanor moved through those rooms — carefully, always a degree of careful that a woman shouldn't need in her own home. The way she'd make a joke and glance at Marcus before she finished it, taking the measure of his reaction. The way she'd sometimes catch Julian's eye across the table with the brief, acknowledging expression of someone who doesn't need to say a thing out loud because someone else is already seeing it.

He had seen it. He had filed it away and said nothing because there was nothing to say, and that had been correct, and it had cost him something every time.

He read on.

---

The third paragraph of the second page was where the letter became something Julian had not been prepared for.

He would later think that he should have been prepared for it — this was Marcus, after all, Marcus who had arranged everything, Marcus whose love expressed itself through the architecture of contingency. But there is a difference between knowing a man's character and anticipating the specific, devastating application of it, and when Julian read the third paragraph his hands went still on the paper.

*I know you're in love with her.*

Just that, to start. The simplest possible statement of the thing Julian had spent five years managing. Then:

*I've known for some time. Three years, perhaps. I watched you at my table and I understood what you were doing — the discipline of it, the way you held yourself slightly away from her at exactly the right distance, close enough to be a friend and far enough to be honest. I was angry, at first. Then I wasn't, because what I understood, the longer I watched you, was that you were choosing the right thing every single day, and choosing the right thing every single day when the wrong thing is available to you is one of the hardest things a person can do. I respected you for it. I still do. I want you to know that.*

Julian set the letter down.

He got up and washed his coffee cup. He stood at the sink with his hands on the counter and the water running, not washing anything, just standing. Then he turned off the tap and went back to the table.

*Now here is what I am asking you.*

He read the next passage standing up, because he had known, somehow, that he needed to be standing.

*When Eleanor is ready — not now, not for some time, but when — I want you to be available to her. Completely. I mean this the way you're understanding it, and I don't want to obscure it with careful language, because careful language is how I failed her and I'm not going to fail her in my last letter. I mean that if she reaches for you — if she comes to you wanting comfort, wanting closeness, wanting the kind of physical intimacy that grief steals from you — I want you to be there for her. Not because you were told to. Because you love her, and because she deserves to be loved by someone who will look at her the way I know you look at her when you think no one is watching.*

*I want her to be touched like she matters. I want her to be wanted in the way that a woman who has spent years being managed needs to be wanted. I could not always give her that. You can. And if she chooses it — this has to be her choice, Julian, her initiation, her pace, I cannot stress this enough — then I need to know that you will not retreat into honor and let her reach for something and find nothing there.*

*I am asking you not to step back from her anymore.*

Julian stood at the kitchen table and read the passage again and felt the specific feeling of a man for whom the thing he has been most careful about for years has just been handed to him by the person he was being careful for. It was not relief. It was not joy. It was something more complicated and more physical than either of those — a loosening of a thing he had kept tightly wound for so long that the loosening felt almost like pain, the sensation of returning circulation.

He sat down.

There was a postscript, four sentences, written in a slightly different pressure than the rest of the letter, as though Marcus had added it last and had pressed harder because he wanted to make sure it was understood:

*She will feel guilty. She will tell herself that wanting anything is a betrayal. The most important thing you can do, before anything else, is make sure she understands that it isn't. She needs to hear that from someone she trusts. You are someone she trusts. Use that.*

---

Julian spent the remainder of the morning with the letter in his hands and no particular awareness of time passing.

He thought about Eleanor at the wedding — not a thing he usually allowed himself with any precision, but the letter had opened something in him and he was not immediately capable of closing it again. She'd worn something simple and white, which had surprised him; he'd expected something architectural, Marcus would have had opinions about the dress, and Eleanor had worn something simple that Marcus's opinions had apparently not prevailed against. She'd carried a single stem of something he didn't know the name of, something pale and slightly wild-looking, wrong for a formal ceremony and exactly right for Eleanor. She'd walked down the aisle with her chin slightly up, in the manner of a woman taking deliberate stock of a situation, and when she'd reached the altar and looked at Marcus the expression on her face had been — Julian had catalogued it at the time and had never fully stopped thinking about it — an expression of enormous, careful love, and something under it that was not quite certainty.

He had been the best man. He had made the toast. He had danced with her twice, which was the correct number, and he had held her at the correct distance, and he had been glad for her, and that was all true, and also at the same time as all of it being true he had driven home at one in the morning and sat in his flat for a long time with the lights off.

He thought about Marcus writing this letter. At some desk — his study at the estate, most likely, the desk facing the east window, which Marcus used for the things he wanted to think clearly about. Writing three pages to his closest friend asking him to love his wife after his death. The deliberateness of it. The months of consideration that must have preceded it. The particular form of love that plans for your own absence with this much care.

It was, Julian thought for the first of many times that day, the most loving thing Marcus had ever done.

It was also, he thought in the same breath, the most Marcus thing Marcus had ever done — the arrangement, the thoroughness, the assumption that the best form of care was a managed one, that love expressed itself most fully as architecture. Marcus had built the conditions of his wife's future comfort the same way he'd built everything else: with specifications, with contingencies, with people placed in positions to fulfill their designated function when the time came.

Julian turned this over for a long time. He was aware that he could read the letter as a gift and he was aware that he could read it as a continuation of Marcus's controlling habits wearing the costume of generosity, and he was further aware that both readings were partially correct and that he was not the one who got to decide which reading mattered. Eleanor was.

That was, in the end, the thing he came back to. *The choice is hers entirely.*

Marcus had written it as an instruction. Julian received it as the one part of the letter he did not have to struggle with. Of course the choice was Eleanor's. That had always been the thing that mattered. Julian's five years of restraint had been built entirely around Eleanor's right to be in the marriage she had chosen without the additional weight of knowing what he felt. The letter didn't change that framework — it just removed one of its walls.

He folded the letter and put it in the inside pocket of his jacket, pressed against his chest.

He went for a walk. He walked for an hour without a destination, which was not a thing he ever did, and ended up on the embankment watching the river do its gray indifferent work, and he thought about how many times he had stood in the Voss house and talked himself back to the correct side of a line, and what it meant that the line had just been moved.

---

At the estate, Cecile Martin read her letter in the room above the kitchen — the desk by the window overlooking the kitchen garden, the space she'd used for seven years to balance household accounts and write grocery orders and do the private work of her interior life. It was her room within the house, her small sovereignty, the place where she got to be herself inside someone else's walls.

She had been expecting something. Marcus Voss did not leave things unfinished. She had managed his household long enough to know the texture of his thoroughness, and she had known in the days after his death that his thoroughness would not stop at dying. It was not that kind of trait.

She broke the seal and read her name in his handwriting and felt the weight of the envelope before she unfolded the pages.

His letter to her was warmer than she'd expected. He thanked her first — for seven years of care, for managing the house with what he called *an intelligence that made everything effortless without ever making it feel managed,* which was a precise and generous description of the work she had done and she received it fully. Then he wrote about the marriage, and about Eleanor, with the specific honesty of a man who had decided that clarity was the last gift available to him.

He had known things about his wife that he had watched from a distance without knowing how to address. He named them directly in the letter: the loneliness, the slow diminishment that happened when someone loves you in ways that make you smaller, the way Eleanor had developed the habit of taking up exactly the amount of space she was given rather than the amount she deserved. He wrote: *I watched it happen and I told myself it was the natural accommodation of two people building a life together and I knew that was not entirely right and I did not correct it and I am sorry for that.*

Then he wrote about what he was asking.

Cecile set the letter on the desk when she reached this part and looked out the window at the covered garden beds. She had known this was coming — not specifically, not the particular shape of it, but the direction.

He had written:

*Eleanor has not been fully herself in a long time. Part of that is my doing. But part of it is also that she has never had — within our marriage, within the life we built — someone who offered her tenderness without a claim attached to it. You have been the closest thing to that, Cecile, and I think on some level she knows it, and I think she has not let herself have it fully because it would have felt like taking something from the marriage. That constraint no longer exists.*

*I am asking you to be present with her. I am asking you to give her what you have always had available for her and what she has not been free to fully receive. If she wants warmth, give it. If she wants tenderness, give that. If she wants, in her own time and entirely at her initiation, something more intimate than that — I want you to know that I have no claim on her that supersedes her own desire. She is a free woman. She is allowed to want things. She is allowed to reach for comfort in whatever form brings her back to herself. What I am asking of you is that you do not hold back from her because of me.*

*She needs someone who will tell her that wanting is not a betrayal. I think that message will mean most coming from you.*

Cecile read this passage twice and then folded the letter and held it in both hands and sat quietly.

She was not surprised by her own feelings for Eleanor — she had examined them with the directness she applied to most things and had arrived at: *yes, real, and no, not actionable, because Eleanor is married and this is her home and certain lines mean something.* She had filed it away. She had managed it the way you manage something true that cannot be used.

Now the line had been moved. And the person who'd drawn it in the first place had been the one to move it.

She thought about Eleanor at the window this morning — standing in the library at six forty-five, watching the courier come up the drive, her face in the particular configuration of someone receiving information through the surface of their eyes and processing it somewhere well below speech. She thought about the way Eleanor's hand rested around a coffee cup, the way she held things with her whole hand rather than her fingertips, the specific quality of how she listened — her whole body tilted toward you, as though listening were a physical commitment.

She thought about all the times she had made Eleanor tea and set it beside her without touching her, the practiced restraint of someone who keeps their hands careful.

The complication, Cecile understood, was not whether she wanted to follow Marcus's instructions. She did, but not because he'd asked — because Eleanor deserved to be tended to and Cecile had always wanted to be the one to do it. The complication was what it meant to act on an invitation from a dead man. Whether following his instructions was liberation or still a form of his management, the hand extended even after he was gone.

She thought Eleanor would be the one to resolve that question, when she was ready to ask it.

She put the letter in the top drawer of her desk and went downstairs to cook.

---

Thomas Vane read his letter in the greenhouse.

He'd known something was coming — there'd been a quality to the morning, the particular tightness in the air that precedes change, and he'd been at the back gate when the courier arrived and taken the envelope without ceremony. He read it standing at the workbench, in the amber light of the heat lamps, with rain beginning to work at the glass.

Marcus had written his letter to Thomas in fewer words than the others. He understood Thomas. You said a thing once, clearly, and Thomas heard it in full. You did not need to build scaffolding around it.

*You've watched her for four years and you have been correct to say nothing, and I want you to know I saw that and thought better of you for it. What I'm asking is straightforward. She needs someone to remind her that her body is still hers — that it is alive and capable of feeling things that aren't grief. She spends too much time in her head, in the library, in the past. You have a way of bringing her back to the present tense, to the physical world, to the sensation of her feet in actual ground. Keep doing that.*

*If she ever reaches for more than presence — if she comes to you wanting something physical, something that is about her body and not her grief — give it to her. Not because I'm asking. Because she'll have chosen it, and she deserves to have the thing she chooses. You know how to be with a woman without making her feel managed. That is rarer than you think, and she needs it.*

*Be patient. Be present. Let her lead. You already know how to do all three.*

Thomas read the letter once and folded it.

He stood at the workbench with his hands resting on the surface. He was not a man who processed things in language — he processed them in the body first, in a kind of understanding that arrived through the chest and settled before it had words. What he felt now was the specific feeling of being seen accurately by someone who was no longer there to be thanked for it.

He had wanted Eleanor Voss for four years with the quiet and unspectacular constancy of someone who understands that wanting and having are different things and that the understanding is not a defeat. He had watched her at the window every morning for four winters and had found reasons to be working in her line of sight not because he was manipulating her attention but because being in proximity to her was one of the reliable goods of his day, the same way the quality of the morning light at six forty-five was a reliable good, simple and not complicated by anything he required from it.

He would do what he would have done anyway. He would be present. He would let her set the pace of everything. He would not take anything she hadn't offered, and if she offered it — when she was ready, if she was ever ready — he would give her what she came for without making her feel that receiving it was a transaction.

He put the seedling trays back in their rows, methodical and unhurried. He checked the heat lamps. He latched the greenhouse behind him and walked back through the rain.

Eleanor was at the library window. He raised his hand. She raised hers.

He drove home when the light was gone, with Marcus's letter against his chest and a particular kind of patience settling into him — not the absence of wanting, but wanting held carefully, the way you hold a tool you're not ready to use yet.

---

Julian called Eleanor at eight o'clock. She didn't answer. He had known she might not and had called anyway because not calling would have been its own statement and he wasn't ready to make it.

He stood at the window of his flat for a while with the phone in his hand and the city outside doing its evening business, indifferent to the particular revolution that had occurred inside his chest today. He thought about Marcus writing those three pages. He thought about the passage he had read nine times, the one that began *I know you're in love with her,* the one that ended with the sentence Julian had been carrying against his skin all day like a burn: *I am asking you not to step back from her anymore.*

He had spent five years stepping back. He had been good at it. He had built the step-back into the architecture of himself so thoroughly that he wasn't entirely sure, standing at the window, what he was going to be like without it.

He got his keys.

He drove to the Voss estate without having decided to drive there — or rather, he had decided somewhere beneath the level of official decision, in the part of him that had been running below the surface all day since the second reading of the letter. He drove with the specific focused silence of a man who is not allowing himself to think about what he is doing so that he can continue doing it, and arrived at the main gate at nine forty-seven to find it exactly as he'd known it would be: iron, unlocked, the drive visible beyond it and the house at the far end with three windows lit.

He turned off the headlights and sat in the dark.

The library window was one of the lit ones. He had been in that room enough times to map it exactly: the high ceilings, the long sofa in green leather, the shelves that Marcus had organized on a system no one else fully understood. He could picture Eleanor on that sofa with her legs tucked up and a book she wasn't reading, in the lamp's pool of light.

He thought about Marcus's instruction with clinical precision, which was the only way he could think about it yet. *If she reaches for you.* The conditional. The fact that the thing being described was contingent on Eleanor's want, Eleanor's initiation, Eleanor's choice. Julian was, in this letter, an option rather than a directive. An available thing. A door that Marcus had asked him to leave unlocked.

He sat with the image of that for a while — himself as an unlocked door — and felt the inadequacy of the metaphor and could not improve on it.

What he felt, underneath the analysis, was an enormous and specific want that he had kept well-managed for five years and that was now, after a day of reading and re-reading, no longer behaving. He wanted to walk up the drive. He wanted to knock on the door and have Eleanor open it and look at him with those gray-green eyes that were always doing their private accounting, and he wanted to tell her the thing he'd been not-telling her for five years, and he wanted to see what happened in her face when he did.

He wanted, which was the simplest and most direct version of everything.

He wanted, and Marcus had said the want was allowed now.

And he was sitting in a car outside the gate not going in, because Marcus had also said: *the choice is entirely hers.* And the fact that Julian's want had been given permission did not mean Eleanor's readiness had been summoned. She was three days past burying her husband. She was in the library in the lamp's pool of light not reading a book, and whatever she was feeling in that room tonight was not a feeling that Julian had any right to walk into without being invited.

He was not going to be the man who arrived uninvited.

He turned on the engine. The lights came back on and the drive went sharp and ordinary before him, and the house sat at the far end of it exactly as it had always sat, and he backed onto the road and drove away.

He would call at eight o'clock tomorrow. She would answer or she wouldn't. He would talk about the Channel Tunnel. He would talk about the restaurant on the corner and Marcus being wrong about the menu, and he would give her the ordinary warmth of an ordinary phone call, and he would be the thing he had always been — present, consistent, patient — except that now there was a letter in his inside pocket that had named the thing beneath all of that, and the naming had changed the shape of everything, and he was going to have to learn to carry himself differently.

The letter stayed warm against his chest the whole way home.

*She deserves to be wanted in the way that a woman who has spent years being managed needs to be wanted,* Marcus had written.

Julian knew exactly what that looked like. He'd been stopping himself from showing her for five years.

He pressed his hand flat against the letter once, briefly, at a red light — the way you'd press a bruise, to confirm it's real.

Then the light changed and he drove on.

*End of Chapter Two*
