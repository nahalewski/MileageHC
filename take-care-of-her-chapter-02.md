# TAKE CARE OF HER
## Chapter Two: What the Letters Say

---

Julian Ashworth received his envelope at eight forty-three on a Thursday morning, which was three days after the funeral and twelve days after the phone call from Stockholm, and which he would later identify as the moment the straightforward part of his life ended.

He was still in his flat when it arrived. He hadn't been sleeping well — he'd been sleeping badly for two weeks now, the specific sleeplessness of someone whose body knows that something is wrong but whose mind is still performing the labor of processing it — and he'd been at the kitchen table since six with a coffee he'd stopped tasting and a newspaper he hadn't opened. He heard the post come through the slot. He didn't move.

He let it sit on the floor for eleven minutes. This was not indecision. Julian Ashworth was not, as a general rule, indecisive. He was a man who processed information before acting on it, which was a quality Marcus had admired in him and which he had himself come to understand as sometimes a strength and sometimes an avoidance mechanism wearing the clothes of a strength. The eleven minutes were, he was aware, the second kind.

When he finally got up and crossed to the door, the envelope was the first thing he saw.

Cream vellum, heavier stock than standard mail, lying with its face up on the doormat among three envelopes of ordinary post and a catalogue he hadn't requested. He recognized the handwriting before he registered what he was recognizing — a reflex, the way you recognize a voice before you've identified whose voice it is. The script was decisive and slightly forward-leaning, the lowercase letters larger than convention suggested, the capital letters restrained. He had read that handwriting in birthday cards and on Post-it notes left on his desk and at the bottom of contracts Marcus had passed across tables at him, and he had not seen it in any of those forms for fourteen days because Marcus Voss was dead.

On the front, below Julian's name and address:

*Take Care of Her.*

He stood on the doormat for a long moment. Then he took the envelope to the kitchen table, set down his coffee cup to make room, and sat with the envelope in front of him. He examined it without opening it. The seal was intact — a pressed wax disk, dark burgundy, Marcus's initials. The kind of thing Marcus did when he wanted to communicate: *this matters, pay attention to the form as well as the content.* He'd seen those seals before. He knew what they meant.

He broke it.

---

The letter was three pages, written on the same cream stock as the envelope, in the same decisive hand. Julian read the first line and put the letter face-down on the table and looked at the wall for a moment. Then he picked it up again.

He would read it twice — once quickly, to understand the shape of it, and once slowly, to let the weight of each part land separately. He had read significant things this way since university, Marcus among those who had first watched him do it and remarked upon it. *You read like a lawyer,* Marcus had said. *Like every sentence is evidence.* Julian had pointed out that he was, in fact, a lawyer. Marcus had said that wasn't the point.

The first reading took four minutes.

Julian set the letter down and stood up and went to the window. His flat was on the third floor and the window looked out over the street — gray morning, the shop across the way not yet open, a woman with a dog working along the pavement with the careful pace of someone who has been up since before the light. He watched her until she was out of view. Then he went back to the table.

He didn't want to read it again. He was going to read it again anyway.

The second reading was different, because the second reading of a thing you don't want to know is where you stop pretending you don't know it.

---

Marcus Voss had been Julian's closest friend for fifteen years. Julian had been the second person Marcus called when he met Eleanor — the first being his mother, who was the first call for everything — and had listened to Marcus describe her with the slightly stunned cadence of a man who is discovering that something he thought he understood has turned out to be more complicated than advertised. *She looked at me like she was deciding something,* Marcus had said. *Like she was already three moves ahead and wasn't telling me what game we were playing.* Julian had asked if that was a good thing. Marcus had said he hadn't decided yet, and then two months later had said yes, definitively yes.

Julian had been the best man. He had written a speech that had taken three weeks of evenings and that he had ultimately folded and kept in his inside pocket and replaced at the podium with something shorter and more honest, something that said: *Marcus Voss is one of the most exceptional people I've known. He loves Eleanor completely. I believe she loves him the same way, and I believe that between the two of them they will make something that holds.* He had said this and meant it and had been entirely right about the most important parts of it, and he had not said everything he could have said, and that was correct, and he had made his peace with it.

Or he had believed he'd made his peace with it. He had, at minimum, successfully behaved as though he had made his peace with it for five years, which he had come to understand was the closest available approximation.

He had been in love with Eleanor Voss since the third time he'd seen her.

This is not the kind of admission Julian made easily, even to himself. He was a man who preferred precision to drama, who distrusted the word *love* when it was applied to things that hadn't been stress-tested, and who had spent considerable private energy over the past five years examining the feeling and looking for the holes in it, waiting for it to reveal itself as something smaller — infatuation, admiration, the particular kind of attachment that develops when you spend a significant amount of time in proximity to someone and mistake familiarity for connection. He'd been waiting for this for five years. The feeling remained.

He'd done nothing about it. He would like to be clear about that, not for credit but for accuracy: he had done nothing, said nothing, implied nothing, acted in no way that could be read as anything other than the behavior of a good friend to a married woman he cared about. There were people who might have called this restraint admirable. Julian called it the only option, because the only alternative was a form of betrayal he was not willing to commit — against Marcus, who trusted him; against Eleanor, who deserved better than that; and against himself, who needed to be able to look at his own behavior without flinching.

He had managed it for five years. He had been careful and consistent and he had attended their dinners and accepted their invitations and been a good and genuine friend to both of them, and he had gone home alone from their house on the evenings when it was the hardest and he had poured himself a drink and he had sat with it until the feeling returned to its normal level, which was: present, manageable, filed.

And now Marcus was dead, and Marcus — who Julian had believed did not know, who Julian had believed he had been meticulous enough that Marcus could not possibly have seen through it — had apparently known the entire time.

The letter was specific on this point.

*You've been in love with her for years. I know. I'm not angry — I was, at the beginning, when I first suspected it. Then I realized that you were also, every day, actively choosing to be a good man about it, which is the kind of thing I can't be angry about even when I want to be. I want you to know I see that. I want you to know it mattered.*

Julian read this passage twice more than any other section. He read it until the feeling it produced in him — something hot and uncomfortable, too complicated to name cleanly — had settled into something he could work with.

Marcus had seen him. He had been keeping this thing locked in the dark for five years, and Marcus had seen it the whole time, and had not said anything, and had decided that what Julian was doing — the restraint, the consistency, the choosing — was worthy of respect rather than confrontation. That was the most Marcus thing Julian had ever encountered. To know, and to evaluate, and to say nothing until he had decided what the knowing was worth.

He had written in the letter what he thought it was worth.

---

Julian spent the rest of the morning not reading the rest of the letter.

This was not accurate, technically — he had read all three pages twice — but what he meant was that there were specific passages he was not yet willing to sit with. He was processing in sections. The section about Eleanor's loneliness — *I know she was lonely inside our marriage, and I know some of that loneliness was my doing, and I am writing this knowing I can't fix it from here* — he had read and then gotten up and washed his coffee cup and stood at the sink for several minutes. The section about what Marcus was asking for — what the three of them had been asked for — he had read and then gone to the window again and stood there long enough to watch the street go from morning-quiet to midday-busy.

The letter was specific. It was, Julian thought, by turns the most loving thing Marcus had ever done and the most presumptuous. It was also, in a way he was struggling to entirely dismantle, absolutely insane. Because what it was asking — what it was *inviting* — was precisely the thing Julian had been disciplining himself against for five years, and Marcus was asking it with the affectionate authority of a man who had arranged everything in advance and was therefore not present to argue with.

There was a particular cruelty in that last part, Julian thought, that Marcus had probably not intended but that existed nonetheless.

He read the relevant passage once more:

*I'm not asking you to pursue her. I'm asking you to stop hiding. She deserves to be loved by someone who is present with her. You've been present for five years and invisible at the same time, and that's — I'm not going to say it's wrong, because I understand why you did it, and it was right for then. But then is over. What I'm asking is that you be honest with her. Not immediately. Not until she's ready. But when she is: be honest. Let her choose. That's all this is. The choice is hers entirely. I just need you to be available for it.*

Julian folded the letter and put it in the inside pocket of his jacket, close to his chest, and went for a walk. He walked for an hour without direction, which was not something Julian Ashworth ever did — he was a man who walked with destinations — and he ended up on the embankment with the river in front of him going about its gray business and the city doing the same on both banks and no closer to knowing what he was going to do than he had been at the kitchen table.

He called Eleanor at eight o'clock that evening, not because he'd decided anything but because he'd promised to call and Julian kept his promises. She didn't answer. He left no message. He would call tomorrow.

He stood in his flat with the phone in his hand for a long time after.

*Be available for it,* Marcus had written.

Julian put the phone in his pocket and got his keys. He had no idea what he was going to say. He drove to the Voss estate anyway.

---

But first: the others.

Cecile Martin read her letter in the room above the kitchen, at the desk by the window that looked out over the kitchen garden, the one she'd been using for seven years to manage the household accounts and write the grocery orders and conduct the private portion of her inner life. She'd been in this room, at this desk, for most of the significant moments of her time at the estate. She had an attachment to it that was less about the room itself than about what the room represented: the place where she got to be Cecile rather than the role, the small sovereignty of a private space in someone else's house.

She had been expecting the envelope.

Not specifically — she had not known there would be envelopes, had not been told — but she had been expecting *something.* Marcus Voss was not a man who left things unfinished. She had spent seven years in proximity to his specific quality of thoroughness, and she had thought, in the days after his death, that his thoroughness would not stop at dying. It was not the kind of trait that had an off switch.

So when the courier came and she saw the cream envelope with Marcus's handwriting on the front, she received it with something that was not quite surprise. She took it upstairs and sat at her desk and read her own name and address in Marcus's script and *Take Care of Her* below it, and she felt the weight of it before she broke the seal.

Her letter was two and a half pages. Marcus's voice came through as it always did when he wrote: direct, unhurried, with occasional precise touches of warmth that she'd learned to recognize as his version of affection — he had not been a demonstrative man, but he had been deliberate, and deliberate warmth from Marcus was worth more than effusion from someone else.

He thanked her first, which was characteristic. He thanked her for seven years of care, for managing the house with what he called *an intelligence that made everything easier without ever making it feel managed,* which was a thing Cecile had worked hard to achieve and was glad to have named. He said he knew she loved the estate and he was glad. He said he knew, also, that she loved Eleanor, and that this was the thing he was writing to address.

Cecile set the letter down briefly when she reached this section. She looked out the window at the kitchen garden — the herbs pulled in for winter, the beds covered and waiting. She picked the letter back up.

He had written about Eleanor with the specific knowledge of a man who had watched his wife closely for five years and understood more than he'd said. He wrote about her loneliness the way you write about something you have looked at from close range and not known how to fix: with regret and without evasion. He wrote about the ways he had, through his own particular habits of possession and control, made the house smaller for Eleanor than it should have been. He wrote: *She needs people who are genuinely present with her, who want nothing from her except for her to be well. I think you've always been that for her. I'm asking you to continue being it, and — if she wants it, and only if she wants it — to be more of it.*

The *more* was the complicated part. Cecile read it and knew what it meant and sat with it for a while, looking at the window.

She was not naive about her own feelings. She had known, for several years, that what she felt for Eleanor went beyond the particular devotion of someone who cared deeply about their work. She had examined it with the direct, unsentimental honesty she applied to most things about herself and had arrived at: *yes, this is real, and no, I am not going to act on it, because Eleanor is married and this is her house and I am her employee, and some things don't cross certain lines regardless of what you feel.* She had made her peace with this the same way you make peace with weather — not by changing it but by learning to work in it.

And now Marcus had read her letter with her, apparently, from across some impossible distance, and had said: *I know. And I'm giving you permission.* And he had said, with the care he'd brought to everything: *but only if she wants it. Let her lead.*

Cecile folded the letter and put it in the top drawer of the desk, under the household accounts, where it would be safe.

Then she sat for a long time looking at the kitchen garden.

The complication wasn't desire — she knew her own desire and had long ago catalogued it. The complication was what it meant to follow instructions from a dead man about how to love his wife. Whether that was a gift or a strange form of continuing control. Whether the two things could even be separated.

She didn't have an answer yet. She thought Eleanor might be the one who'd eventually give it to her.

She went downstairs to make dinner.

---

Thomas Vane read his letter in the greenhouse.

He'd been expecting it too, after a fashion — or expecting something. He'd been at the back gate when the courier came and there'd been a quality to the man's demeanor, a sense of purpose, that had told Thomas before the envelope appeared that this was not ordinary correspondence. Thomas was observant in the way of people who work outdoors: he noticed details before he noticed their significance, catalogued them wordlessly, and allowed understanding to arrive in its own time. He took the envelope. He thanked the courier. He went back to the greenhouse.

He read it at the workbench, standing, in the amber warmth of the heat lamps, with the smell of growing things around him and the rain beginning at the glass. His letter was shorter than the others. Marcus had written to each of them differently, and what Marcus knew about Thomas was that Thomas did not require a great many words to understand a thing. You could say something once, plainly, and Thomas would hear it completely.

The letter said: *You see her. You've always seen her, and you've been correct to keep it quiet, and you should know I noticed that too. What I'm asking isn't complicated. Be there. Be patient. Be honest if she asks you to be. You know how to do all three of those things better than anyone I know.*

Thomas read the letter once, folded it, put it in his jacket.

He stood at the workbench for a while with his hands resting on the surface and the rain working at the greenhouse glass. He was not a man who moved quickly through decisions — he thought of himself as someone who thought the way rivers move, which is slowly and with direction, finding the inevitable path by working with what's there rather than forcing the shape.

The decision he arrived at was not a decision about what he wanted, because what he wanted had been clear to him for some time with the uncomplicated clarity that is one of the few advantages of a straightforward nature. It was a decision about how to proceed, which was a different kind of question.

He would proceed the way he always had. He would be present. He would notice. He would not make demands of the space between them, would not push the intimacy faster than Eleanor wanted it to move, because Eleanor was a woman who needed to find her own pace and he had known this from a considerable distance before Marcus said it in a letter. He would be what he had always been and he would let her choose what she wanted from it.

This wasn't compliance. Thomas did not do things because he was told to. He did this one because it was the same thing he would have decided anyway, and Marcus had simply put the name to it. It felt, in a way that he wouldn't have said aloud, like being recognized.

He put the seedling trays back in their rows. He checked the heat lamps. He latched the greenhouse door behind him and walked back to the house through the beginning of the rain, and he thought about Eleanor at the library window, and he raised his hand, and she raised hers back.

He drove home when the light was gone.

---

Julian parked on the verge outside the Voss estate's main gate at nine forty-seven in the evening.

He hadn't called ahead. He was aware that not calling ahead was a departure from his normal behavior and that it represented something he didn't want to name yet, though it was available to be named. He'd driven from the city in the particular focused silence that comes when you are making a choice you haven't officially acknowledged making, and arrived outside the iron gate with the headlights on and the engine running and the house visible at the end of the drive — three windows lit, the entry hall light, what he knew was the library.

The letter was in his inside pocket. He'd put it there this morning and it had been against him all day, which was its own kind of eloquence.

He sat in the car for a while.

He thought about Marcus. He did this with the particular frequency of recent grief — the thought arriving unprompted, staying longer than you've invited it to, rooting around in the specific memories you'd rather let settle. He thought about Marcus at thirty, when they'd first moved to the city and were sharing a flat in a period that Julian looked back on now as the last time either of them had been uncomplicated. He thought about Marcus at thirty-four, calling him from wherever he'd had dinner with Eleanor for the third time to say: *I think this is it, Jul. I think this is actually it.* He thought about Marcus at the wedding, at the head of the room, looking at Eleanor with the expression of a man who has wanted something for a long time and is only just allowing himself to believe he has it.

He thought about Marcus writing the letters. At some desk, in advance — because it would have been advance, Marcus would never have trusted chance — writing three letters and sealing them and arranging for their delivery and thinking: *this is the right thing. This is the last useful thing I can do.*

It was the most loving thing Marcus had ever done. Julian had thought this repeatedly today and kept arriving at it again. It was also, he had thought equally repeatedly, the most Marcus thing Marcus had ever done — the arranging, the timing, the thoroughness of the care, the assumption that the best way to love someone was to manage the conditions of their wellbeing with sufficient precision that the outcome was likely. That was Marcus. That was his love and also, Julian had watched it over five years, the thing that made Eleanor smaller in rooms where she should have been large.

The tension between those two things was the heart of the letter and the heart of the marriage and possibly the heart of everything that was going to unfold from this point forward.

Julian turned off the headlights. The drive went dark. The house stood at the end of it with three lit windows, not expecting him.

He could go in. The gate was unlocked — it was never locked before eleven — and he could drive up and knock on the door and Cecile or Eleanor would answer and he could say whatever it was you said when you appeared at someone's house uninvited at ten in the evening, and then he could say whatever came after that.

He did not know what came after that. That was the problem.

He was not a man who stepped into conversations without a reasonable sense of where they were going. He used words precisely, which meant he did not produce them without understanding their function. He had spent eleven hours today trying to understand the function of the conversation he would eventually have to have with Eleanor — about the letter, about what Marcus had asked, about the thing he'd been not-saying for five years — and he had arrived at: *not yet. Not now. Not without knowing what she needs first.*

Because what Marcus had said — the most important thing in all three pages, the thing Julian had come back to again and again — was: *the choice is entirely hers.* And Julian did not know yet what Eleanor wanted to choose, and he wasn't going to walk into that house and complicate her grief with a confession she hadn't asked for just because Marcus had told him he was allowed.

Permission was not the same as invitation.

He sat in the car outside the gate for a few more minutes. The house didn't move. The lights stayed on.

Then he started the engine and drove home.

He would call at eight o'clock tomorrow. She would answer or she wouldn't. He would talk about nothing in particular, which was its own form of saying something, and he would wait, because waiting was — as he had come to understand over five years of being meticulous with himself — the only honest thing he had.

The letter stayed in his inside pocket all the way home.

He did not take it out. He did not put it in the glove compartment the way he'd intended. He kept it close, instead — a warm and complicated weight against his chest on the left side, like something alive, like something that had woken up and was now simply waiting.

*The choice is hers,* Marcus had written.

Julian was going to make sure she had time to know what she was choosing.

*End of Chapter Two*
