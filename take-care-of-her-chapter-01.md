# TAKE CARE OF HER
## Chapter One: Three Days After

---

The first letter arrived three days after the funeral. Not to me. To them.

---

I'll tell you about the funeral first, because the funeral is where the story actually begins, even though I didn't know it then. Even though I thought, standing in the October rain with my black umbrella held at a precise angle above my head, that the story had already ended.

Marcus Voss was buried on a Thursday. The service was at eleven in the morning, in a church he'd attended twice: once for our wedding, and once for his mother's burial four years before that. He hadn't been religious. He'd been respectful of religion the way he was respectful of most institutions — with the detached appreciation of someone who understood their social function without requiring their comfort. Marcus believed in systems. Churches were a system. He could respect that.

I chose the church because it was where we were married and I did not have the interior resources to choose anything else. Every decision in the eleven days between his death and his burial had required a negotiation between the functional part of my brain and the part that had simply stopped responding, and the compromise was almost always: *choose the thing that was already chosen.* The church from our wedding. The hymns his mother had liked. The dark green of the flower arrangements, because dark green was the color of the kitchen curtains in the first flat we'd rented together, at twenty-six and twenty-nine respectively, when we were still learning each other and everything felt like a discovery.

He would have found the dark green impractical. He would have said something dry about seasonal floral arrangements. He would have been right, and I would have kept them anyway, and he would have let me, because that is how it worked. That is how it worked.

The church was full. I hadn't expected that. Marcus had been a private man — sociable when the occasion required, selectively warm, generous with money and far more calculated with genuine access. He had a gift for creating the impression of intimacy without surrendering any real part of himself, and I had admired that when we were first together and later understood it as the thing I kept running up against when I tried to reach him in certain rooms of himself that he'd decided were his alone.

I'd thought perhaps sixty people. Seventy-five at most.

Instead: two hundred, at least. Business associates in the front left pews, the ones I recognized from dinners and charity events and the careful machinery of Marcus's professional life. Former colleagues from his early years, when he was still building rather than managing. A contingent from the city — younger, expensive-coated, the sort of people for whom Marcus's death would register as the loss of an opportunity as much as the loss of a man, and I did not begrudge them this because at least it meant he had mattered enough to be mourned in multiple registers.

Then the people who actually knew him. Fewer of those. They were easier to identify because they were the ones who looked genuinely unsteady.

Julian stood two rows behind me and two seats to the left. I knew without looking — I knew the specific quality of his stillness, the way he takes up space when he is holding himself together. I had known it for five years, had categorized it without thinking about why I was cataloguing it, and I felt it now the way you feel weather before it arrives: a pressure change, a shift in the atmosphere around him.

I did not look at him during the service.

The priest said things that were true and things that were general and one thing, near the end, that was surprisingly specific — Marcus had apparently spoken to the priest more than I'd known, in the last year, informal conversations that had taken place while I was elsewhere. I noted this with the surface of my mind and filed it. There was a category in my internal inventory labeled things Marcus did that I didn't know about and it was already full and had been for some time and I had made a practice of not examining it too closely.

I stood straight through all of it. I held my order of service with both hands and kept my face in a configuration that I knew, from the occasions I had practiced it, communicated composed grief without inviting intervention. People are less likely to speak to you if you look like you're managing. I needed people to be less likely to speak to me.

I thought about Marcus across a dinner table, which was not a thought I invited but which arrived without asking, as those thoughts do. The particular way he'd looked up from whatever he was reading — not quickly, but deliberately, like he was choosing to redirect his attention and wanted you to know the choice had been made. Then the quality of his focus when it arrived: complete. That had been one of the things I loved first and hardest about him, that quality of focus. The sense that you were, in the moment he gave it to you, the only relevant thing in the room.

I thought about his hands. I don't know why I kept thinking about his hands.

At the graveside the rain intensified. Not a downpour — a steady, cold, October insistence, the kind of rain that doesn't apologize for itself. I held my umbrella at the precise angle required to keep my face dry while the water ran off the edge onto the back of my coat, and I stood with my feet in the soft ground at the edge of Marcus's family's plot and watched the coffin descend on its mechanical apparatus and understood, at some low register below thought, that this was permanent.

The word *permanent* had no real weight yet. It was still just a word. I could feel it waiting to become something heavier once I was somewhere quiet enough to let it.

Julian appeared at my right shoulder as they finished. He didn't say anything. He stood close enough that his umbrella overlapped mine by several inches and we shared the shelter of both without acknowledging it, and we stood there together until it was finished, and then the crowd began its slow dispersal and I turned and walked back to the car and Julian walked beside me, and we didn't speak all the way to the car, and he held the door for me, and I got in, and that was the most honest exchange of the whole day.

---

Back at the house, I stood in the marble entry hall and accepted condolences for two hours.

This is a skill. You develop it, if you attend enough funerals. You learn to hold someone's hand with the correct pressure — firm enough to be present, brief enough to allow the interaction to complete itself. You learn to receive the specific statements people make when they don't know what to say, the *he was a wonderful man* and the *you were such a beautiful couple* and the one that arrived a dozen times in different formulations: *he loved you so much. You could always tell.* 

I received that one without expression every time it came. I looked at the person who said it and I said thank you and I meant it and I also knew that whatever they had seen from the outside was a partial truth, and partial truths are the kindest kind and so I kept them.

Cecile moved through the rooms with trays. She had prepared everything herself — not out of necessity, because we could have had the service catered, but because Cecile expresses care through food in the same way some people express it through speech, and this was care. I noticed the miniature pastry cases filled with things Marcus had preferred. I noticed the wine was his preferred label. She had planned all of this before the funeral and she had planned it as a form of tribute, and I could not look at the trays directly or I would have to feel something about them.

Thomas had brought in the last roses from the south garden — heavy, blowsy, deep red, the very end of the season's generosity — and arranged them in the entry hall in the way Marcus had always liked, which was: densely, no space between the stems, no minimalism, no apology for abundance. He stood at the back of the room during the gathering. He was not a man comfortable with parties or funerals or any occasion that required the performance of social fluency, but he was there, in his good dark jacket that I'd seen twice in five years, standing at the edge of things with his hands folded in front of him like a man attending a ceremony he takes seriously.

I appreciated that. I filed it.

Julian found me at twenty past one. He'd waited, I noticed, which was characteristic — he had never been someone who pushed toward the center of things. He'd stood at the periphery for an hour while the room thinned, working his way toward me with the patience of someone who has decided that waiting is the form of attention this situation requires. When he finally arrived he looked at me with an expression I didn't try to decode.

"Ellie," he said.

"Julian."

He held both my hands for a moment — long enough to be real, brief enough not to require a response from me. He didn't say *I'm sorry* or *he was a wonderful man* or any of the other things that had been said at me all afternoon. He didn't say anything. He just held my hands and then released them and stood there, present, which was the right thing, which I was not surprised was the right thing for Julian to do.

The guests cleared out by three. Cecile and Thomas moved through the rooms with quiet efficiency, and the house returned to itself, shedding the gathering the way a coat sheds rain.

Julian was the last one.

He stood in the front doorway with his coat on, and the October afternoon was gray behind him, and he had that look he sometimes got where he appeared to be making a decision that he'd already made and was just now acknowledging. I was at the foot of the stairs and I watched him make it and watched him land on the side of going.

"I'll call," he said.

"All right," I said.

He left. The door closed. The house settled.

I stood in the entry hall alone for a while. One of Thomas's roses had shed a petal onto the marble floor — a single dark red comma — and I looked at it for a long time and then stepped over it and went upstairs.

I did not sleep in our bedroom that night.

---

I want to be precise about the bedroom, because it matters to the story and because I have spent some time since then trying to understand what I was actually afraid of.

Marcus had designed it with the specific intention of creating a room that felt complete in itself. He had an architect's sensibility without the training — an instinct for proportion and material and the way objects relate to each other in space. The bed was a California king in dark walnut with linens in the particular white that is not quite ivory, and the windows looked out over the east garden, where he'd had jasmine planted in our first summer because he'd noticed I opened the windows to smell whatever was blooming outside. That kind of attention. That was the thing about him — that meticulous, constant attentiveness that made you feel beheld and sometimes, on the harder days, watched.

In winter the jasmine was gone and the room smelled of wood oil and cedar and, under both of those, something I have no proper name for except *him.* The smell of the pillow on his side of the bed. The particular chemistry of a person's sleep. It had been fourteen days since he'd slept there and the smell was still present, and I knew with absolute certainty that if I crossed the threshold of that room and lay down in that bed, the smell of him would be the last thing I processed before I lost the composure I had been maintaining all day, and I didn't know what losing it would look like or whether I'd be able to find my way back.

So I stood in the doorway for a long moment. The room was exactly as it had been the morning he left for his business trip. His reading glasses on the nightstand, folded. His book face-down on his side of the bed — he'd been reading something dense and economics-adjacent, as he always was, and I'd teased him about it as he packed and he'd said, with the dry affection that was his version of tenderness, that some of us enjoyed reading things that required effort, and I'd laughed and told him to pack a novel for the flight home, and he'd taken a novel off the shelf and put it in his bag with the exaggerated compliance of someone indulging a request they find charming.

The novel was still in his bag. His bag was at the hotel, I supposed, or had been collected, or was somewhere in the category of *Marcus's things that need to be dealt with* that I hadn't opened yet.

I closed the bedroom door.

The library is at the end of the east corridor — a long room with high ceilings and casement windows and the two walls of built-in shelves Marcus had added in our second year, which had required three weeks of construction work and a level of organizational planning that I had found both excessive and entirely predictable. His shelving system was elaborate and opaque: organized first by acquisition year, then by a private satisfaction rating that lived only in his head, then by some tertiary principle he had explained to me once at length and that I had mostly failed to absorb, not because I wasn't listening but because it was the kind of explanation that assumed a familiarity with the system you were supposed to be learning.

There were books I recognized and books I'd never seen him read and books that were clearly aspirational — the ones you buy because you intend to become the version of yourself who has read them. He'd had more of those than most people would have expected. I found that comforting, in some dim and private way.

The sofa was a chesterfield in dark green leather, long enough to sleep on, which I had discovered inadvertently in the first year of the marriage when a late reading night had become a sleeping night without my noticing the transition. I got a blanket from the linen cupboard and a pillow from the spare room and I arranged myself with the practiced efficiency of someone who has already made peace with what they're doing, and I lay in the dark and looked at the ceiling and waited.

I waited for grief. I waited for the collapse I'd been braced for since the phone call from the hospital in Stockholm, when the doctor's voice had come through the line in careful, accented English and everything in my life had reorganized itself in a moment around a single fact. I'd been bracing for fourteen days and the collapse hadn't come, and I was starting to understand that my particular grief was not going to announce itself. It was going to work through me differently. I didn't know the form it would take. I was waiting to find out.

Sleep came first. Which seemed wrong, but there it was.

---

The next day had the quality of a room with all the furniture in the wrong positions. Everything was technically correct — I moved, I ate, I drank coffee, I stood at windows, I exchanged words with Cecile and with Thomas in passing — but the spatial relationships were off. The distances between things felt wrong. The air in each room had a different character than it should have.

I moved through it anyway.

The house in grief is a particular kind of place. It was forty-seven rooms, and I had known all of them in the mode of *our house*, and now I was beginning to know them in the mode of *my house*, which was a different knowledge in the way that the same words in a different language are a different thing. The study, which I passed without entering, felt like a held breath — as if the room itself was waiting for Marcus to come back and work and the waiting had a texture you could feel through the closed door. The dining room was theatrical in its stillness: a stage set for a production that had been cancelled. The kitchen was closest to normal, because the kitchen had always been more Cecile's than ours, and Cecile was still there, and the kitchen still smelled of bread and coffee and the herb garden she kept in pots on the sill.

I ate both meals in the kitchen. Cecile sat with me both times. She talked about ordinary things — what Thomas needed to do to the south garden before the first hard frost, a delivery of wine that had arrived and needed to be properly catalogued and stored, something procedural from the estate attorney that would require a signature next week. She spoke about these things without apology, without the hushed, slightly theatrical reverence that some people adopt around fresh grief as though grief is a sleeping animal they're afraid to disturb.

I was grateful for this in a way I couldn't quite access directly. It sat in my chest like a pressure I hadn't known was there until something relieved it slightly.

Julian called at eight o'clock. I looked at his name on the screen and felt the impulse to answer followed immediately by the understanding that I had, precisely, nothing to say — that I was empty in a specific way, hollowed of the ordinary social material that makes speech possible, and that trying to fill that emptiness with words I didn't have would be worse than silence. I set the phone face-down on the sofa cushion and listened to it ring until it stopped.

A text arrived twenty minutes later: *Just checking in. Call when you're ready. No rush.*

I put the phone in the kitchen. I went back to the library. Outside the casement windows the October night had arrived fully — cold and starless, the kind of dark that takes the shape of the garden you know is out there even though you can't see it.

I thought about *no rush* as a statement. The generosity of it. The patience it encoded. I didn't have many people in my life who offered patience without condition, without the implicit expectation that the patience would eventually be rewarded by whatever they wanted from you. Julian had always been different in this way, and I had always known it, and I had filed that knowledge in a drawer I didn't open.

---

The second day was nearly the same, except for the quality of my attention, which had shifted in some small degree from inward to outward. I noticed things I had not been noticing. The specific way morning light entered the conservatory at eight forty-five and made the glass prismatic — this had been happening every clear October morning for five years, and I had appreciated it and moved past it, and now I stood in it for several minutes and did nothing else.

I noticed that Thomas arrived at seven-fifteen each morning and that the first thing he did, every morning, was walk the full perimeter of the grounds. I had known this abstractly — I'd been told, at some point, that he did it, that it was a practice — but I had never actually watched it happen from beginning to end. From the library window I watched him make the full circuit of the estate: south garden to rear boundary to east wall to north terrace and back. He walked it deliberately, pausing occasionally to examine something close, his breath making small white clouds in the cold. It took twenty-two minutes. He made no adjustments, only observations. Then he went back to work.

Marcus had described Thomas as a man who understood the difference between patience and passivity. I had not thought about that description since Marcus said it, and I thought about it now.

When Julian called at eight I answered on the second ring.

"How are you?" he asked.

"The same," I said, which was accurate.

"I know it's still early," he said. "For anything to be different."

"Yes."

There was a pause, and I could hear him deciding something — Julian's silences have a texture that I have learned to read, the same way you learn to read weather by looking at the sky rather than waiting for the rain — and then he said: "I watched a documentary tonight. About the Channel Tunnel. Did you know the boring machines were named? The women operating them gave them names. Margaret. Virginia. Brigitte."

I was quiet for a moment. Then: "Marcus would have found that interesting."

"He would have had a proprietary feeling about it," Julian said. "He would have felt a personal connection to any large-scale, extremely expensive underground engineering project."

Something moved in my chest — not quite laughter, but the structural memory of it, the shape of it. "He would have wanted to know the budget," I said.

"And then the actual budget. The overage on the budget."

"And who was responsible for the overage."

"And whether they'd been held accountable," Julian said, and his voice had warmth in it that pressed against something in me, and I turned slightly away from it because I wasn't ready, but I didn't end the call.

We talked for forty-three minutes. About nothing important and then about less than nothing and then, near the end, about a restaurant near his office that had closed six months ago, a place Marcus had declared overpriced on two separate visits and that Julian and I had always, privately, agreed was exactly right.

"He was wrong about that restaurant," I said.

"He was occasionally wrong," Julian said carefully.

"He knew when he was wrong," I said. "He needed private time to acknowledge it before he said so out loud."

"Yes." Julian's voice was different for a moment — the controlled quality dropped and underneath was something unfiltered and raw, something he was not offering to me, just experiencing where I could hear it. "That's exactly right. That's exactly him."

We were both quiet for a moment.

"I'll call tomorrow," he said.

"I know," I said.

And I did know, with a certainty that had no logical basis after two phone calls but was absolute nevertheless. Julian would call at eight o'clock the following evening and the evening after that. He would do this not because he'd promised to and not because he was following instructions — I didn't know yet about the instructions — but because this was a commitment he had made to himself without articulating it, and Julian kept the commitments he made to himself with the same rigor he brought to everything else.

I went back to the library after the call and lay on the sofa in the dark with my eyes open and thought about nothing in particular, which was a kind of relief, and fell asleep eventually with all the lights still burning.

---

The third day.

I was at the library window at six forty-five when the courier came up the drive. It was already light — barely, the particular gray light of late October that arrives without warmth — and the courier was a young man in a gray jacket, professional and precise in the neutral way of people employed to transfer things between parties without becoming part of the story. He drove a white car. He carried a leather satchel worn comfortable with use. He moved up the front steps with the even gait of someone with eleven more deliveries to make before lunch.

Cecile opened the front door before he knocked. She must have been watching for him, or perhaps the security camera notification had come through to her phone the way it came through to mine when the long drive was in use. Either way, she was there, and she accepted the envelope from him with a brief exchange I couldn't hear through the glass.

The envelope was cream. I could see, even from the second floor, that the handwriting on the front was done with care. Cecile held it with both hands for a moment after the courier left. She stood in the open door and looked at it, and the cold air came in around her, and she did not move.

She didn't open it.

This was the first thing I noticed. The standing. The both-hands. The not-opening of an envelope she'd been, apparently, expecting. People open their letters in the doorway when the post arrives — it's an ordinary reflex, the small private ceremony of correspondence. Cecile stood in the doorway with the envelope in both hands and looked at it as though she was deciding something that the letter had not yet demanded she decide, and then she went back inside and the door closed and the drive was empty again.

I watched the courier's white car descend to the road and turn and disappear between the hedgerows.

Something in my attention had altered. I was aware of it the way you're aware of a shift in air pressure before a storm — not alarming, not yet, but notable. I'd been functioning for eleven days on a kind of narrowed perception, everything filtered through the gauze of shock and managed grief, and something about the delivery and Cecile's stillness in the doorway had brought the edges of things into sharper focus.

I looked at my book. I was not reading it and had not been reading it.

Thirty-one minutes later — I checked the time without deciding to — another car appeared on the service road along the south garden.

The service road is visible from the library's east-facing window if you're standing near the glass rather than sitting on the sofa, so I was standing near the glass when the second courier parked and walked along the path toward the back gate. Thomas was already there, as Thomas was always already there at any hour, in any light, in any weather. He was working the far end of the east bed with his back to the path, and he turned when he heard the gate, and the courier crossed to him and produced another envelope.

Thomas took it. He held it for a moment, looking at the front of it, and I watched his expression from a distance that was too great for the specifics but not too great for the overall: something settling in him, something that was not surprise. He read, or began to read, and then stopped and folded the letter back into the envelope with a deliberateness that was almost ceremonial. He put it inside his work jacket, tucked close against his chest, and buttoned the jacket over it.

He stood still for a moment. His face was turned slightly away from me. Then he went back to the east bed, and the courier returned to his car, and the service road was empty.

I stayed at the window.

I was doing the arithmetic. Two envelopes, two people. Both received at the same time on the same morning, three days after Marcus Voss was put in the ground, which meant: sent in advance, scheduled for delivery, prepared by someone who had planned the timing. Who planned everything. Who I had been married to for five years and who was, I was now understanding, still capable of surprising me.

By the time I had worked through this arithmetic I already knew about the third envelope. I didn't need to wait for confirmation. I knew there was a third one because the three people closest to the operation of my daily life were Cecile and Thomas and Julian, and Marcus — meticulous, thorough, exhaustively prepared Marcus — would not have sent two letters and left out the third.

Julian would have received his envelope that morning at his flat in the city. He would read it the way he read everything, with the focused attention he applied to information that required decisions, and then he would fold the letter and hold it for a moment in both hands, the way Cecile had, because whatever was in the envelopes required a moment of holding before you could put it down.

I stood at the library window and looked out at the garden.

Thomas had resumed his work at the east bed, his movements the same careful unhurried movements as always, but he had not checked his phone. He had not looked toward the house. He was doing what Thomas always did, which was be completely present in the physical space immediately in front of him, and that presence now had something in it that it hadn't had before the envelope arrived. Something that had been given a shape by whatever was inside.

The rose beds in the south garden were stripped for winter — just the canes now, cut back and tied, waiting in their rows with the patience of things that know they'll come back. The greenhouse at the far end of the garden was gold with interior warmth, its glass panels fogged with the difference between outside and in.

Marcus had planted the roses. He had had very specific opinions about roses. He had been, in fact, specific about nearly every aspect of this garden, which was one of the ways the garden was his even though I was the one who spent time in it and Thomas was the one who maintained it. Marcus owned things through attention. He owned them by caring about them in detail. I had been one of the things he owned in that way and I had found it various things over the years: flattering, suffocating, moving, exhausting, and occasionally, in the best moments, the thing that made me feel most real.

At eight o'clock Julian called.

"I got a letter today," he said, before I could say anything at all.

"I know," I said.

A pause. "How do you know?"

"I watched the others receive theirs this morning," I said. "From the window. What does it say?"

A longer pause than the first one. The length of it told me something. Julian's pauses are economical — he doesn't pause to think, generally, because he thinks before he speaks, which means when a pause does appear it's carrying content.

"I think we should talk about it in person," he said finally. Carefully.

"That bad?"

"Not bad." He seemed to consider the word. "Not simple. It's — not simple."

"He was never simple," I said.

"No," said Julian. And then, very quietly, almost to himself: "No, he really wasn't."

I looked out at the garden. The path lights had come on in the dimming of the late afternoon, outlining the gravel paths between the beds with a low amber glow. The greenhouse held its warmth at the far end. Thomas had finished for the day; the garden was empty, just the roses and the paths and the last light going.

"Come Saturday," I said. "We'll talk then."

"All right. Saturday."

After we hung up I stayed by the window for a long time. I wasn't looking at anything in particular. I was thinking about Cecile in the doorway and Thomas with his jacket buttoned over whatever Marcus had written to him and Julian's voice going careful in the way it goes careful when he's decided to tell me less than he knows.

Three envelopes. Three people. Three days after, timed — because Marcus timed things.

I turned the facts over, examining them with the flat, analytical attention that is my most reliable tool and also my most reliable defense. I arranged what I knew and looked for the shape of it.

He had written to the three people who were closest to the daily life of this house and to me. He had written to them after his death, through arrangements he must have made before it. He had timed the delivery to the third day after the funeral, which was specific enough to be intentional. Whatever he'd asked of them — whatever the letters contained — was significant enough that Cecile had stood in the doorway deciding something, and Thomas had held the letter against his chest, and Julian had said *not simple* the way you say it when the complication is large.

And he had not written one to me.

Not to me.

That, I understood, was also intentional.

I told myself the letters were about the house. The finances, the estate management, the practical architecture of a life that needed to continue running without the person who'd designed it. That made sense. That was the kind of thing Marcus would have arranged, the thorough handover documentation of a man who took nothing for granted.

I told myself that, and I looked at the garden, and I turned the sentence over, and I felt, for the first time since the funeral, something other than the flat suspension of managed grief.

I felt curious.

I was almost right about the letters. Almost.

---

*End of Chapter One*
